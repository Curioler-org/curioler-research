## At a glance

**Sample size:** 56 school-based intervention studies in the anchor review, read alongside a 2025 review of 233 empirical studies and several randomised school trials  
**Demographics:** Autistic pupils in mainstream primary and secondary schools, mostly aged 5-18, predominantly children with spoken language  
**Key outcome:** Across 56 school-based intervention studies, interventions increased the accessibility of school activities to autistic pupils, but reciprocity and friendship between autistic pupils and their peers were not necessarily improved.  
**Methodology:** A systematic review of school-based interventions that targeted at least one level of the school environment - the autistic child, the peers, the staff, or the physical environment - and measured the quantity or quality of autistic pupils' social participation. This is read alongside a 2025 systematic review of 233 empirical studies on inclusive education and randomised trials of social skills groups delivered inside schools.  
**Researchers:** Yung-Ting Tsou and colleagues (2024 review), Valentine Perrelet and colleagues (2025 review), Connie Kasari and colleagues (school-based randomised trials)  

**Limitations:**

- The reviews pool studies with very different designs, ages and outcome measures. The 2025 review of 233 studies found no consensus on how school inclusion should even be measured, which makes effects hard to compare across studies.
- Most of this research involves children with spoken language in mainstream classrooms. Children with higher support needs, minimally speaking children and special-school settings are much less represented.
- Many individual studies are small single-case or single-site designs, and even the largest randomised school trials here involve fewer than 150 autistic children.
- Outcomes are usually measured over weeks to a few months, so very little is known about whether school-based social gains hold across school years.
- Studies rarely evaluate implementation - whether school staff keep delivering a programme as designed once the researchers leave.
- Almost all of this literature measures adult-defined social participation. Autistic pupils' own accounts of what a good school day feels like are only recently being collected systematically.

## What this research found

- Being in a group is not the same as being in the group. Classroom social network research found that fewer than half of autistic children were involved in their classroom's social networks, and that they were more likely to sit on the periphery or be isolated at every grade level. They were not more likely to be actively rejected, but they were less accepted and had fewer reciprocal friendships than matched classmates.
- The largest review of school-based inclusion work makes the same point about interventions. Across 56 studies, programmes increased autistic pupils' access to school activities, but reciprocity and friendship with peers were not necessarily improved. Access went up; connection did not automatically follow.
- Working on the child alone is the approach that most consistently fails. In a randomised trial that crossed a peer-mediated approach with a child-assisted approach, teaching the autistic child social skills made little difference to their actual engagement with peers - it only became effective when paired with work that involved the peers themselves.
- There is no single best format for a school social group. A later randomised trial of 137 autistic children aged 6-11 compared a didactic skills group with an activity-based group and found no overall winner. What worked depended on the child's relationship with their teacher: children with low teacher closeness or high conflict gained more from the structured skills group, while children who already had a close teacher relationship gained more from the activity-based group.
- Where the support happens matters as much as what it is. The review noted that most school interventions were delivered outside the ordinary classroom routine, in a separate room - and a 2025 meta-analysis of social problem-solving interventions found that teacher-led, school-based delivery produced stronger effects than researcher-led sessions outside school contexts.
- Programmes built into unstructured social time show some of the clearest gains. Recess-based work that coaches school staff to set up activities around a child's interests and support peers to join in has increased time spent engaged with peers in randomised trials, with gains maintained at follow-up, and a 2025 review of 20 recess studies found peer-mediated and adult-facilitated approaches to be the most promising.
- The classic pull-out social skills group has modest effects that travel poorly. A meta-analysis of 55 school-based social skills studies found low treatment effects and low generalisation across people and settings, though gains that were made tended to be maintained. A Cochrane review concluded there is some evidence that social skills groups improve social competence for some children, with mixed results overall and little evidence about quality of life.
- Most of the field asks the child to change rather than the school. A 2025 review of 233 studies found that interventions overwhelmingly targeted autistic students' own social skills and behaviour, with far fewer attempts to adapt the school environment, and almost none addressing the physical environment.
- School is not a neutral setting, and for some children it is a harmful one. A scoping review of school absenteeism found attendance rates of 68.5-80% for autistic pupils against 89-93% for pupils generally, with emotionally based school avoidance accounting for 43-55% of absence and bullying identified as a risk factor. Reviews of autistic young people's own accounts of mainstream secondary school describe high levels of stress, anxiety, loneliness and frustration.

## What this means for caregivers

- If you are told that school will "bring your child out of themselves" simply by putting them with other children, treat that as a hopeful theory rather than a finding. The evidence says proximity to peers, on its own, is one of the least reliable ways to build your child's social life.
- The active ingredient in the research is organised peer involvement - peers who are briefed, activities chosen around your child's interests, an adult who sets things up and then steps back. It is reasonable to ask a school specifically who is working with the peer group, not only what is being taught to your child.
- Ask where support happens. Gains show up most consistently when help is delivered inside real school moments - the playground, lunch, group tasks - rather than only in a separate room at a separate time. A skill practised in a quiet side room may simply not appear at break time.
- Ask how progress is measured. "He joined in well in group" is a report about the group room. The measures that mattered in these trials were things like who children actually played with at recess and who named them as a friend.
- Your child's teacher is not just context, they are part of the intervention. In one trial the best approach differed depending on how close or conflicted the teacher-child relationship was, which is a strong argument for treating a warm, well-informed class teacher as a priority rather than a bonus.
- Weigh the cost side honestly. Attendance, sleep, how your child is after school, and whether they are willing to go are legitimate evidence about whether the school day is helping. High distress and rising absence are not signs of a child failing at school so much as signals that the setting is not currently working for them.
- None of this settles the mainstream-versus-specialist question for your child. What the evidence does suggest is that the label on the placement predicts far less than the quality of the peer support, staff understanding and environment inside it.

## Important caveats

- The specific question - whether attending school works as a form of group therapy - has never been tested directly, and realistically cannot be, because there is no ethical trial that assigns some autistic children to school and others to no school. Everything here is evidence about what happens to social outcomes within school, which is the closest available answer, not the same question.
- Evidence that friendship gains are hard to produce is not evidence that school is bad for autistic children's social development. Many autistic children do form friendships at school, and some of the most valued ones are with other neurodivergent children, which most of these measures capture poorly.
- These results come mostly from mainstream settings in high-income countries, especially the UK, US, Netherlands and Australia. Class sizes, support staffing and playground culture vary enormously, and so will how any of this plays out.
- Bullying figures quoted in this area often come from studies covering neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions as a whole rather than autistic children specifically, so treat single percentages with care.
- Widely shared statistics on school distress - such as the finding that most children experiencing school distress are neurodivergent - come from surveys of families already in difficulty. They describe who is affected, not how common the problem is across all autistic pupils.
- A child who looks well included may be masking. Autistic adults consistently describe having spent school years suppressing distress to fit in, and observational measures of participation cannot distinguish comfortable belonging from effortful compliance.
- If your child is refusing school, losing skills, or showing exhaustion that does not lift at weekends, that is worth raising with your GP, paediatrician or educational psychologist rather than managing alone.

## About this research

The anchor source is a 2024 systematic review in the Review Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders that pooled 56 studies of school-based interventions aimed at socially including autistic pupils in mainstream schools, and found that access to activities improved while reciprocity and friendship often did not. It is supported by a 2025 systematic review of 233 empirical studies in Autism showing that the field overwhelmingly targets the autistic student rather than the school environment, by two randomised trials of social skills groups run inside schools, by a meta-analysis of 55 school-based social skills studies and a Cochrane review of social skills groups, and by a scoping review of autistic school absenteeism. Taken together the design of this evidence base is stronger on whether specific programmes work than on whether school as a whole is socially good for autistic children - which remains, in the strict sense, an unanswered question.
