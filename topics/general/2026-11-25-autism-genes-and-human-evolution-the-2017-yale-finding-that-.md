## At a glance

**Key outcome:** Spikins and colleagues argue that from about 100,000 years ago, cooperative human groups made room for people with autistic-style skills such as exceptional memory, detail focus and an understanding of natural systems. No direct evidence identifies any ancient person as autistic.  
**Methodology:** A peer-reviewed archaeological hypothesis paper (2016) and a peer-reviewed genetic analysis (2017); this summary draws mostly on essays, a press release, a book chapter and blog posts about them, not the full papers.  
**Researchers:** Penny Spikins, Barry Wright, Derek Hodgson, Renato Polimanti, Joel Gelernter  
**Institutes:** University of York, Yale School of Medicine  

## What this research found

- The idea has a long history. In 2006, a psychiatrist said in an interview that human evolution was driven by slightly autistic people. Spikins later wrote that she would not go that far. She had published an archaeology paper on autism and the origins of modern human behavior in 2009 and a book chapter in 2013. Her 2016 paper with Wright and Hodgson, a University of York press release and a 2017 magazine essay brought it to a wider audience. The 2017 Yale genetic paper added a separate line of evidence from DNA.
- The main argument is about groups, not single people. Spikins says human success came from differences between people, which allowed specialist roles. Her examples include exceptional memory, sharp sight, taste and smell, and a deep understanding of animal behavior. One 2005 study of a Siberian reindeer herder found he remembered the parentage, medical history and character of each of his 2,600 animals. He was more comfortable with reindeer than with people, yet he was respected and had a family. Spikins also points to inventions that appear after about 100,000 years ago, such as spear-throwers, multi-part harpoons and tiny stone blade tools. She adds engraved bone plaques that seem to track the phases of the moon, and strict, rule-based food sharing among modern Arctic hunters.
- Cave art is her other main clue. Paintings such as those at the Chauvet Cave in France are very realistic and detailed, and they focus on parts rather than wholes. Spikins notes that these traits appear in some talented non-autistic artists too, but are much more common in talented autistic artists. She says this suggests autistic perception influenced the art, not that the painters were autistic.
- On genes, Spikins writes that some autism-linked genes are part of a heritage shared with other apes and others are over 100,000 years old. About a third of autism cases come from new, spontaneous genetic changes, while the rest tend to run in families. A summary of twin studies puts how much of autism is down to genes at roughly 64% to 91%. These points support the idea that autism is old. They do not show why it persisted.

## What this means for caregivers

- This research offers a view of autistic ways of thinking as one long-standing form of human variety, not only a modern problem. Some caregivers find that perspective affirming.
- It does not predict anything about a particular child. The talents it discusses, such as focus, memory and pattern-spotting, are not found in every autistic person. Support needs are real and vary widely.

## Important caveats

- No one can diagnose autism from bones, tools or paintings. Spikins herself has said that you cannot say some cave art was drawn by someone with autism, only that some traits fit. Posts claiming science has proven that autistic artists shaped early culture go well beyond what the papers say.
- The genetic and archaeological ideas are not yet joined up. A critique of the viral claims notes that no study shows specific autism-linked variants were selected because of the social shift Spikins describes. A sign of selection also does not say what the benefit was, if any. Another overview says several competing explanations exist and none has clearly won, and it warns how easy it is to tell just-so stories. The sources here barely describe the Yale study's methods, so its strength could not be judged.
- Spikins' own work lists limits. Severe autism may never be an advantage. Autistic traits showed up unevenly across time and place, appearing, fading and returning in southern Africa. She also suggests that some hunter-gatherer groups developed ways to limit anyone with rigid, dominating behavior. Her 2013 chapter said key autism genes were missing from Neanderthal DNA. A later 2024 study reportedly found some Neanderthal-derived variants enriched in autistic people, a finding the sources here do not explain.
- Much of what was read here was an essay, a press release, a book chapter or a blog post about the papers, not the papers themselves. Most of the talents discussed are described mainly in autistic people with fewer support needs, so the story does not speak for every autistic child.

## A Curioler thought

The deep past is pieced together from a few bones, tools and painted walls, and the rest is careful imagination. It is humbling and a little thrilling that we are still learning how many different kinds of minds it took to build the world we live in.
