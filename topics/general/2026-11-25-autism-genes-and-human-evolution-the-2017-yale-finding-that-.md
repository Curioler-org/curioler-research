## At a glance

**Key outcome:** Spikins and colleagues argue that from about 100,000 years ago, cooperative human groups made room for people with autistic-style skills such as exceptional memory, detail focus and an understanding of natural systems. No direct evidence identifies any ancient person as autistic.  
**Methodology:** A peer-reviewed archaeological hypothesis paper (2016) and a peer-reviewed genetic analysis (2017); this summary draws mostly on essays, a press release, a book chapter and blog posts about them, not the full papers.  
**Researchers:** Penny Spikins, Barry Wright, Derek Hodgson, Renato Polimanti, Joel Gelernter  
**Institutes:** University of York, Yale School of Medicine  

## What this research found

**The puzzle.** Autism runs strongly in families. Twin studies suggest roughly 64% to 91% of it comes down to genes. Some autism-linked genes are shared with other apes, and others are more than 100,000 years old. That raises a simple question. If these genes made life harder, why did they not fade away over thousands of generations?

**The clue from DNA.** In 2017, Renato Polimanti and Joel Gelernter at the Yale School of Medicine looked at common gene variants linked to autism. They found widespread signs of what geneticists call positive selection. In plain words, over a very long time, people who carried some of these variants tended to leave more descendants, so the variants spread. That hints that, in the right setting, they brought something useful.

**The clue from the Stone Age.** Penny Spikins, an archaeologist at the University of York, came at the same question from the past. Her 2016 paper with psychiatrist Barry Wright and Derek Hodgson argues that about 100,000 years ago, human groups became much more cooperative. People shared food by firm rules, looked after one another and relied on each other's strengths. In a group like that, you did not have to be good at everything. A person who found small talk hard but could remember every animal track, spot a tiny flaw in a stone blade or keep count of the moon's phases could be valued for what they did best, and be looked after in return.

**What those people may have given the group.** Spikins points to inventions that appear around and after this time: spear-throwers, harpoons built from several parts, and tiny, precisely shaped stone blades. She also points to bone plaques with marks that seem to track the moon, an early kind of calendar. Each of these needed long focus, patience with detail and a feel for how things work. She also looks at cave art, such as the paintings in Chauvet Cave in France. They are strikingly realistic and focus on fine parts rather than the whole scene, a style much more common among talented autistic artists today.

**A living example.** Spikins draws on a 2005 study of a reindeer herder in Siberia. He knew the parentage, medical history and character of every one of his 2,600 reindeer. He was more at ease with animals than with people, yet his community respected him, and he had a family. For Spikins, he shows how a mind like this can be a real asset to a group that makes room for it.

**The big idea, in one line.** Human success may have come not from everyone thinking alike, but from groups that included and supported very different kinds of minds, autistic minds among them.

**How the idea grew.** The idea has been around for a while. In 2006, a psychiatrist said in an interview that human evolution was driven by slightly autistic people. Spikins has said she would not go that far. She published on autism and the origins of modern human behavior in 2009 and in a 2013 book chapter, *The Stone Age Origins of Autism*. Her 2016 paper, a University of York press release and a 2017 essay in SAPIENS brought the idea to a wide audience. The Yale genetics paper came out the following year and added evidence from DNA.
## What this means for caregivers

- This research offers a view of autistic ways of thinking as one long-standing form of human variety, not only a modern problem. Some caregivers find that perspective affirming.
- It does not predict anything about a particular child. The talents it discusses, such as focus, memory and pattern-spotting, are not found in every autistic person. Support needs are real and vary widely.

## Important caveats

- Showing that autism genes are old is not the same as explaining why they stayed. About a third of autism cases come from new, spontaneous genetic changes rather than inherited ones, so an evolutionary story can only ever explain part of the picture.
- No one can diagnose autism from bones, tools or paintings. Spikins herself has said that you cannot say some cave art was drawn by someone with autism, only that some traits fit. The same detail-focused style also turns up in some talented non-autistic artists. Posts claiming science has proven that autistic artists shaped early culture go well beyond what the papers say.
- The genetic and archaeological ideas are not yet joined up. A critique of the viral claims notes that no study shows specific autism-linked variants were selected because of the social shift Spikins describes. A sign of selection also does not say what the benefit was, if any. Another overview says several competing explanations exist and none has clearly won, and it warns how easy it is to tell just-so stories. The sources here barely describe the Yale study's methods, so its strength could not be judged.
- Spikins' own work lists limits. Severe autism may never be an advantage. Autistic traits showed up unevenly across time and place, appearing, fading and returning in southern Africa. She also suggests that some hunter-gatherer groups developed ways to limit anyone with rigid, dominating behavior. Her 2013 chapter said key autism genes were missing from Neanderthal DNA. A later 2024 study reportedly found some Neanderthal-derived variants enriched in autistic people, a finding the sources here do not explain.
- Much of what was read here was an essay, a press release, a book chapter or a blog post about the papers, not the papers themselves. Most of the talents discussed are described mainly in autistic people with fewer support needs, so the story does not speak for every autistic child.

## A Curioler thought

The deep past is pieced together from a few bones, tools and painted walls, and the rest is careful imagination. It is humbling and a little thrilling that we are still learning how many different kinds of minds it took to build the world we live in.
