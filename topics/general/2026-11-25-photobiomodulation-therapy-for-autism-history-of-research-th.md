## At a glance

**Sample size:** 30 children in the 2024 randomized trial (16 real light, 14 fake light); 22 children finished the 2025 open-label follow-up (25 enrolled)  
**Demographics:** Autistic children ages 2–7 in New York City. In the 2024 trial, mean age about 4.6–4.9; 24 boys and 6 girls; 22 of 30 White; 18 verbal and 12 non-verbal; most had moderate-to-severe autism ratings. Children with a seizure history or regular medication were excluded. The 2025 study had mean age 4.95, 19 boys and 3 girls, 86% White, and 16 verbal children.  
**Key outcome:** Children who got real light had a 7.23-point larger drop in their CARS-2 autism rating than the fake-light group (95% CI 2.36–12.11, p=0.011).  
**Methodology:** A randomized, double-blind trial with a look-alike fake device in 30 children, plus a single-group follow-up in 22 children, both published in peer-reviewed journals with the device maker involved.  
**Researchers:** Yuliy Fradkin, Eugenia Steingold, Luis De Taboada, Margaret Naeser, Anne Saltmarche, Wayne Snyder, Joaquin A. Anguera, Alexander J. Simon  
**Institutes:** JelikaLite Corp (device maker, New York), Rutgers University (author contact address; Statistics Department helped analyze EEG data)  

## What this research found

**The idea.** Photobiomodulation is light, usually red to near-infrared (about 600 to 1300 nanometers), applied to the body. One researcher first described it in 1967, when a low-power laser made hair grow faster on mice. Scientists have since studied light on the head for brain injury, stroke, depression and dementia. A 2022 review by Catherine Hamilton, John Mitrofanis and colleagues argued that light might help in autism too. Light is absorbed in mitochondria, the tiny energy factories inside cells. They suggest it could boost cell energy, calm inflammation in the brain, and rebalance how distant brain regions talk to each other. They called it an idea worth testing.

**The first steps in people.** A small 2022 pilot with 11 adults with high-functioning autism reported fewer autism traits after eight weeks of pulsed near-infrared light on the forehead. A research group led by Calixto Machado reported that children had less irritability after four weeks of low-level laser treatment. The 2022 review says those gains lasted up to 12 months. A 2022 look back at records of children treated in Italy reported lower autism ratings, less rigid behavior and better sleep.

**What the 2024 trial tested.** A team led by Yuliy Fradkin and Eugenia Steingold ran the first randomized trial of their wireless 500-gram cap, called Cognilum, at two New York City offices from March to October 2021. It has six small LEDs that pulse invisible 850-nanometer light 40 times a second. They aimed it at the default mode network, a set of brain areas linked to social thinking, at the language areas known as Broca and Wernicke, and at the back of the head. Thirty children ages 2 to 6 were assigned by a random-number generator to the real cap or a fake one. The fake cap looked and acted the same but gave off no light. Sessions were twice a week for eight weeks. Light time was built up slowly to 5 or 6 minutes to avoid making children hyperactive. Children could walk around, play, or watch cartoons. Families, children and the clinician who scored the ratings did not know who got real light. About three quarters of the children kept up their usual therapies.

**What changed.** In the real-light group, the average autism rating fell from 43.5 to 33.7. In the fake-light group it fell from 40.6 to 38.0. The researchers used 4.5 points as the smallest drop experts consider meaningful. By that measure, 14 of 16 children (87%) with real light improved, compared with 5 of 14 (35%) with fake light. Parents also took part in weekly phone interviews with staff who did not know the group assignments. They rated more positive change in the real-light group (median score 20 vs 16). The authors report that parents of children who had mild side effects chose to continue because they saw better eye contact, focus, and understanding and use of language.

**A look at brain waves.** Seventeen of the 30 children could tolerate an EEG cap, which records the brain's electrical activity through sensors on the scalp. Over the eight weeks, slow delta waves tended to drop and theta waves tended to rise more in the real-light group. The overall theta difference between groups was statistically significant (p=0.049). Children whose autism ratings fell most also tended to show the biggest drop in delta waves and the biggest rise in theta waves. The authors suggest this may be an early sign of the light's effect. They say it needs much more study.

**The 2025 follow-up and what families noticed.** The same group then treated 22 children ages 2 to 7 (average 4.9) with the same device. Every child got real light for eight weeks, and no child got fake light. Average ratings fell from 36.45 to 29.68, about the same size of drop as the real-light group in 2024. EEG recordings showed fast gamma waves rising and slow delta waves falling over the sessions. The authors say their rating scale did not capture some changes that mattered most to families. Examples were holding hands while crossing the street and using the bathroom independently.

**Where it goes next.** The authors and reviewers agree on the next steps. One is larger, more rigorous trials run by independent teams. Another is working out the best dose, including pulse rate, session length and number of weeks. The 2024 authors suspect longer sessions may be needed for older children or those with darker skin, because more light is absorbed before it reaches the brain. They also want longer follow-up, brain imaging, other measures of behavior and family life, and checks on how the light interacts with medicines. Hamilton and colleagues also want animal studies to test why light might work, and they float ideas such as daily light helmets and effects through the gut. In short, the light seems to be worth testing carefully.

## What this means for caregivers

- Near-infrared light for autism is an early research idea, not an established therapy. The studies showed signs worth following up, but they cannot say yet whether it truly helps children or for how long.
- The trials tested one investigational device at specific settings, with short sessions run by a research team. Nothing in this research shows that red-light caps, panels or mats sold for home use act the same way.
- In the trials, children kept their usual therapies. The question being studied is light as an add-on, not a replacement. Your child's pediatrician or developmental specialist is the right person to talk through any new approach with.

## Important caveats

- **Small and early.** Only one study compared real light with a fake cap, and it had just 30 children from two New York offices, most of them White. The 2025 follow-up gave every child real light, so there was nothing to compare it with.
- **Run by the company that makes the cap.** JelikaLite paid for the 2024 study and helped design and analyze it. Some of the authors work for the company or own part of it. No outside team has repeated the results yet.
- **Other things could explain some of the change.** The children who got real light started with higher scores, and more of them did not yet use spoken words. Children also grow, keep up their usual therapies, and parents hope. The study lasted only eight weeks, so no one knows if any change lasts. The brain-wave results are early hints, not proof.
- **Side effects were mild, but only checked for a short time.** Some children became overexcited or had headaches. In the 2025 study, most parents saw their child become overactive at least once. Children with seizures or on regular medicine were left out, so the results do not tell us about them.

## A Curioler thought

Every brain is a small universe of cells, each one turning fuel into the energy behind a thought. Science is only starting to ask whether gentle light can reach that quiet work. We don't know yet, and that is part of the wonder: there is still so much to learn about the minds we love.
