## At a glance

**Sample size:** 25 randomised controlled trials pooled (4 methylphenidate, 4 atomoxetine, 1 guanfacine, 14 antipsychotic, 2 other); a second 2025 review covered 32 studies  
**Demographics:** Children and young people under 25 with a diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder, across trials published from 1992 onwards  
**Key outcome:** Methylphenidate reduced hyperactivity with a standardised mean difference of -0.63 (parent-rated) and -0.81 (teacher-rated); atomoxetine reduced parent-rated hyperactivity by -0.49 and inattention by -0.54. Evidence quality was graded low or very low.  
**Methodology:** Systematic searches of databases and trial registries, pooling randomised placebo-controlled trials with a random-effects model; hyperactivity and inattention were measured using parent and teacher rating scales. A second, PROSPERO-registered 2025 systematic review covered both drug and non-drug interventions in under-18s with both diagnoses.  
**Researchers:** Rebecca Rodrigues, Meng-Chuan Lai, Evdokia Anagnostou, Peter Szatmari, Stephanie H Ameis, Carmela De Domenico, Francesca Cucinotta  
**Institutes:** Western University, Canada, Centre for Addiction and Mental Health, Toronto, The Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto, Autism Research Centre, University of Cambridge, IRCCS Centro Neurolesi Bonino-Pulejo, Messina  

**Limitations:**

- Only a handful of trials existed for each medicine (4 for methylphenidate, 4 for atomoxetine), and most were small and short
- Overall quality of evidence was rated low to very low, so future trials could change these numbers
- No trial examined what happens with long-term continued use, which is how these medicines are usually taken
- Most outcomes rest on parent and teacher questionnaires rather than direct observation
- Non-drug approaches appeared in only 4 of the 32 studies in the 2025 review, so their effect in this specific group is largely unknown
- Trials rarely included autistic children with significant intellectual disability or minimal speech, so findings may not transfer to every child

## What this research found

- Hyperactivity in autistic children is most often studied as co-occurring ADHD, which is common in autism and adds meaningfully to day-to-day difficulty.
- Methylphenidate, a stimulant, produced the clearest reductions in hyperactivity (moderate to large effects on both parent and teacher ratings) and smaller reductions in inattention.
- Atomoxetine, a non-stimulant, reduced inattention and parent-rated hyperactivity, and in some studies also reduced stereotyped behaviour and social withdrawal. More children stopped atomoxetine than methylphenidate because of side effects.
- Extended-release guanfacine has a smaller evidence base but reduced hyperactivity, irritability and repetitive behaviour in a placebo-controlled trial of 62 children and in a 2025 multi-centre clinic study of 29 children; it is often used when stimulants are not tolerated.
- Second-generation antipsychotics also reduced hyperactivity, but only indirect evidence supported this and their side-effect burden is heavier.
- Non-drug options were strikingly under-studied. In a trial comparing atomoxetine, parent training and their combination, parent training alone did not separate from placebo on core ADHD symptoms over four weeks, though a 24-week extension found benefits for challenging behaviour at home.
- Side effects across trials clustered around sleep difficulties, reduced appetite, irritability and stomach upset, with drowsiness and dizziness more typical of guanfacine.

## What this means for caregivers

- If hyperactivity is a major difficulty for your child, it is reasonable to ask whether a co-occurring ADHD assessment would be useful, since that is the door most of this evidence sits behind.
- Medication is not a single decision: methylphenidate has the strongest pooled effect, while atomoxetine and guanfacine are alternatives when stimulants are not tolerated. Which one fits depends on your child's wider profile.
- Expect a try-and-review approach rather than a guaranteed result. Effects in these studies were real but not dramatic, and a meaningful minority of children stopped because of side effects.
- Ask your clinician what will be monitored and when. Appetite, sleep, irritability, weight, heart rate and blood pressure all appear in these trials as things worth watching.
- Because long-term data is missing, periodic review of whether a medicine is still helping is part of good care, not a sign that something has gone wrong.
- Thin research on parent training and behavioural approaches for this specific group does not mean they do not help; it means they have not been properly tested here. Environmental supports such as predictable routines, planned movement breaks and sensory-aware spaces remain reasonable to pursue alongside any medical route.

## Important caveats

- This is a summary of research, not medical advice. Decisions about medication belong with your child's doctor, who knows their full history.
- The evidence was graded low to very low quality. That does not mean these medicines do not work, but that the studies were few, small and short, and conclusions could shift.
- Effect sizes come from rating scales completed by parents and teachers, which capture impressions rather than objective measurement.
- Hyperactivity can also be driven by sleep problems, anxiety, pain, sensory overload or an unsuitable environment. Those causes deserve attention before or alongside medication.
- Children in these trials were often those without significant intellectual disability, so the findings may not describe every child's likely response.

## About this research

The anchor source is a practitioner review published in the Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry (2021), which systematically searched databases and trial registries from 1992 onwards and pooled 25 randomised controlled trials using a random-effects model. It is supported by a 2025 PROSPERO-registered systematic review in the Journal of Clinical Medicine covering 32 studies of drug and non-drug interventions, and by a 2025 retrospective multi-centre study of guanfacine in Scientific Reports. The two reviews agree closely, which strengthens confidence in the direction of the findings even though both rate the underlying trial quality as low.
