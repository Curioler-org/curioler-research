## At a glance

**Sample size:** Cochrane review of 7 small trials of sound therapies; 2025 systematic review that screened 4,741 records and found only 8 eligible studies across 6 different sound-based interventions; supporting evidence from small physiological studies and single-case reports  
**Demographics:** Autistic children and young people. The main paediatric screening tool for sound intolerance (PMHQ) is designed for ages 6–17, so an 11-year-old sits squarely inside the assessed age range  
**Key outcome:** No single treatment removes sound sensitivity. The strongest evidence supports a combined approach — identify which kind of sound intolerance your child has, use ear protection for specific loud situations rather than all day, adjust the environment, and use CBT-style graded exposure — while Cochrane found no evidence to support auditory integration training or similar sound therapies  
**Methodology:** Synthesis of a Cochrane systematic review of auditory integration training and other sound therapies, a 2025 systematic review of sound-based interventions in children, review papers on the definitions and prevalence of decreased sound tolerance in autism, small physiological and attention studies of noise-attenuating devices, and NHS and National Autistic Society clinical guidance  
**Researchers:** Zachary J. Williams and colleagues (definitions and mechanisms), Y. Sinha and colleagues (Cochrane review of sound therapies), Vincent, Skaczkowski, Hughes-Barton & Gunn (2025 systematic review), Pfeiffer and colleagues (noise-attenuating headphones)  
**Institutes:** Cochrane Collaboration, Vanderbilt University Medical Center, University of South Australia, Temple University, NHS audiology services (UK), National Autistic Society  

**Limitations:**

- There is still no randomised controlled trial of any treatment for sound sensitivity carried out specifically in autistic children — most of the direct evidence is small studies and single cases
- The 2025 systematic review found only 8 usable studies across 6 different interventions, with high variation in design, population and outcomes measured
- Much of the practical guidance about limiting ear-defender use comes from audiology clinical consensus rather than trial data
- Prevalence figures vary widely (roughly 40% to 70%) because studies define and measure sound intolerance differently
- Almost nothing is known about which children respond to which approach, or about long-term outcomes into adolescence

## What this research found

- **Sound sensitivity is common, and it is not one single thing.** Researchers group it under the umbrella term *decreased sound tolerance* (DST), which an estimated 50–70% of autistic people experience at some point in life. Underneath the umbrella sit three different problems: **hyperacusis** (ordinary sounds are physically too loud or actually painful), **misophonia** (specific trigger sounds — chewing, sniffing, tapping, a sibling's breathing — provoke anger, disgust or rage rather than pain), and **phonophobia** (fear of a sound that might happen, producing anticipatory anxiety and avoidance, such as refusing to enter a building because the fire alarm *might* go off). Most children have a mix, but the balance matters, because the three respond to different things.
- **The first step in the research literature is assessment, not treatment.** Reviews consistently recommend an audiological assessment before any intervention: to rule out ear or hearing problems that can masquerade as sensitivity, and to measure loudness discomfort levels (the volume at which sound becomes uncomfortable) so there is a baseline to measure change against. A parent-report screening tool, the Paediatric Misophonia and Hyperacusis Questionnaire (PMHQ), was developed for children aged 6–17 with and without autism specifically to tell these subtypes apart early.
- **Ear defenders and noise-cancelling headphones genuinely help in the moment — and can make things worse if worn all day.** A small study using wearable sensors found that noise-attenuating headphones reduced sympathetic nervous-system activation (measured through skin conductance) in autistic children with auditory hypersensitivity, and a 2024 study found auditory attention improved with noise-cancelling earbuds. Occupational therapy guidance supports them for immediate relief and for getting a child into places they would otherwise avoid. But NHS audiology services and specialist school guidance are consistent and blunt about the flip side: constant use causes the brain to turn up its own internal gain, so ordinary sounds feel *louder* once the protection comes off. Their advice is to reserve protection for specific genuinely loud events and to reduce habitual use gradually, not abruptly.
- **Auditory integration training and similar 'sound therapies' have no evidence behind them.** The Cochrane review of auditory integration training — the Berard-style programmes, and related listening therapies, often sold as multi-session packages — found seven small studies with mixed results, with benefit reported in only two studies involving 35 participants across two outcomes. The reviewers' conclusion was that there is no evidence to support the use of auditory integration therapy or other sound therapies, that they must be considered experimental, that care must be taken not to risk hearing loss, and that parents should be aware of the cost involved. A 2025 systematic review of sound-based interventions in children reached a similarly cautious verdict: preliminary signals of benefit for auditory sensitivity, anxiety and behaviour, but with heterogeneity so high the authors called for proper randomised trials before drawing conclusions.
- **Graded exposure delivered in a CBT framework has the most credible track record.** Cognitive behavioural therapy with an exposure and habituation component is the established evidence-based approach for hyperacusis, misophonia and tinnitus distress across the wider clinical literature, and a 2025 review lecture summarising that evidence found both psychologist- and audiologist-delivered CBT reduced the impact of these conditions on quality of life. In autism specifically the direct evidence is thinner: a published case report of a modified CBT approach for an autistic child with hyperacusis — combining graded exposure, sensory self-regulation strategies and adaptations for the child's communication and processing style — reported improved loudness discomfort levels and reduced reliance on noise-cancelling headphones in daily life. One case is not proof, but it is the direction the evidence is pointing.
- **Environmental adjustment does much of the practical work.** Guidance from the National Autistic Society treats sensory adjustments as a school's reasonable-adjustment duty rather than a favour: advance warning of fire drills, permission to wear ear defenders for bells and assemblies, staggered arrival or departure to miss the corridor crush, seating away from speakers and noisy classmates, exams in a quiet room, a designated calm space the child can leave for without asking permission, and small physical fixes like felt pads on chair legs. These are the changes that most reliably reduce daily distress while any therapeutic work is going on.

## What this means for caregivers

- **Start by working out which kind of sound sensitivity your child has.** Keep a simple two-week log: which sounds, in which settings, and what the reaction looks like — hands over ears and pain, or anger at one specific person's chewing, or refusing to enter a room in case a sound happens. That distinction is what tells an audiologist or psychologist which approach to try, and it is information only you can collect. Ask your GP or paediatrician for an audiology referral to rule out hearing or middle-ear issues first — the research is unanimous that this comes before treatment.
- **Treat ear defenders as a tool for specific situations, not a uniform.** They are the right answer for a fireworks display, a school assembly, a busy shopping centre, a fire drill. If your child currently wears them most of the day, the research does not say to snatch them away — it says to reduce use gradually while keeping anxiety low, ideally with an audiologist's or occupational therapist's help, and to replace all-day protection with quieter environments and planned quiet breaks instead.
- **Be sceptical of anything sold as a sound-therapy programme.** If a provider offers a fixed course of listening sessions through headphones to 'retrain' your child's hearing — auditory integration training, Berard, Tomatis-style programmes, listening therapies — the Cochrane review found no evidence they work, flagged a hearing-safety concern, and specifically flagged the cost to families. That money is better spent on an audiology assessment or CBT sessions.
- **Eleven is a good age for the talking-therapy route.** Graded exposure works better when a child can understand the plan, agree to it, and rate their own discomfort — which most 11-year-olds can do with adaptations. Look for a psychologist or audiologist experienced with both autism and sound intolerance, and expect the approach to be modified: visual scales, predictable steps the child controls, and self-regulation strategies built in alongside the exposure.
- **Put the school adjustments in writing.** Advance warning of fire alarms, permission to leave for a quiet space, seating away from noise, and exams in a separate room are standard reasonable adjustments — not special pleading. Getting them recorded in your child's support plan means they survive a change of teacher.
- **Take the anxiety component seriously.** By this age, a lot of the daily disruption comes from anticipating sounds rather than hearing them, and avoidance shrinks a child's world quickly. Anxiety that has grown around sound is treatable in its own right, and treating it is often what makes the exposure work possible.

## Important caveats

- This is a research summary, not a treatment plan. Sound sensitivity that is new, one-sided, painful, or accompanied by ear pain, tinnitus or hearing changes needs a medical assessment rather than a sensory strategy — see a GP or audiologist.
- There is no medication with established evidence for treating sound sensitivity in autistic children. Medication is sometimes used for co-occurring anxiety, which is a different target and a decision for a prescribing clinician.
- The evidence base here is genuinely thin. The strongest single conclusion — that auditory integration training does not work — is a negative one, and the positive recommendations rest on clinical consensus, small studies and case reports rather than large trials in autistic children.
- The warning about ear-defender overuse comes from audiology clinical practice, not from randomised trials, and it has to be balanced against the real access it buys some children. Withdrawing protection abruptly, or refusing it in genuinely loud environments, is not what the guidance recommends.
- Sound sensitivity varies enormously between children and across time in the same child. What works in a quiet supermarket at 8am may fail at 5pm, and that is a feature of the condition rather than a sign the strategy is wrong.

## About this research

This summary draws on the strongest available evidence rather than a single study: a Cochrane systematic review of auditory integration training and other sound therapies, a 2025 systematic review of sound-based interventions in children from the University of South Australia, review papers by Williams and colleagues that established the current hyperacusis/misophonia/phonophobia framework and prevalence estimates for autism, small physiological and attention studies of noise-attenuating headphones and earbuds, a 2025 review of CBT for sound-intolerance conditions, and clinical guidance from NHS audiology services and the National Autistic Society. The quality is uneven: the Cochrane conclusion is solid, but no randomised trial has yet tested a full treatment approach specifically in autistic children, so several of the practical recommendations rest on clinical consensus.
