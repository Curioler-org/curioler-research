## At a glance

**Sample size:** The Cochrane team reviewed 7 small studies of sound-therapy programs. A 2025 review sifted through 4,741 papers and found only 8 solid enough to include. The rest of what we know comes from small studies and individual cases  
**Demographics:** Autistic children and teenagers. The main questionnaire used to work out what kind of sound sensitivity a child has was built for ages 6 to 17, so an 11-year-old sits comfortably in the middle of it  
**Key outcome:** Nothing makes sound sensitivity disappear. What helps most is a combination: find out what kind of sound problem your child actually has, use ear protection for specific loud events rather than all day, make the environment quieter, and build up tolerance slowly with the child leading. The one clear negative finding is that auditory integration training and similar listening programs do not work  
**Methodology:** This pulls together the strongest evidence available rather than one study: a Cochrane review of listening programs, a 2025 review of sound-based approaches for children, research papers on how common sound sensitivity is in autism and what forms it takes, small studies of ear defenders and noise-canceling earbuds, and NHS and National Autistic Society guidance  
**Institutes:** Cochrane Collaboration, Vanderbilt University Medical Center, University of South Australia, Temple University, NHS audiology services (UK), National Autistic Society  

**Limitations:**

- Nobody has yet run a proper large trial of any treatment for sound sensitivity specifically in autistic children — most of the direct evidence is small studies and single cases
- The 2025 review found only 8 usable studies covering 6 different approaches, and they were too different from each other to compare properly
- The advice about not wearing ear defenders all day comes from what audiologists see in clinic, not from a trial
- How common sound sensitivity is depends on who you ask — estimates range from about 40% to 70% because studies measure it differently
- We do not know which children respond to which approach, or what happens to them long-term

## What this research found

- **Sound sensitivity is common, and it is not one single problem.** Roughly 50 to 70% of autistic people struggle with everyday sounds at some point. But "struggling with sound" covers three quite different things. Some children find ordinary sounds truly too loud, even painful. A hand dryer or a scraping chair actually hurts. Some are set off by particular sounds rather than loud ones: chewing, sniffing, a sibling humming, someone tapping a pen. Here the reaction is anger or disgust, not pain. And some are frightened of a sound that *might* happen. They won't go into a hall in case the fire alarm goes off, or they avoid a birthday party because of balloons. Most children have a mix, but which one leads changes what will help.
- **Get a hearing check before you try anything.** Every review says the same thing: see an audiologist, a hearing specialist, first. Partly this rules out ear infections, glue ear (fluid behind the eardrum) or hearing problems that can look like sensitivity. Partly it gives you a starting measurement, so you can tell later whether anything has really changed. There is also a questionnaire parents fill in, designed for ages 6 to 17. It helps sort out which of the three kinds of sound problem your child has.
- **Ear defenders help in the moment. Wearing them all day slowly makes things worse.** Both halves of that have support. In one study, children wore sensors that track how the body reacts. While wearing headphones that cut out noise, they were measurably calmer, with less of the racing-heart, on-alert response. A 2024 study found children could pay attention better with noise-canceling earbuds in. Occupational therapists, who help children cope with everyday tasks and places, recommend them for getting a child into places they would otherwise refuse. But NHS audiology departments (UK hospital hearing clinics) are blunt about the other side. If a child's ears are covered all day, the brain makes up for it by turning up its own internal volume. So ordinary sounds feel *louder* than before once the defenders come off. Their advice is to save them for specific loud events. If a child already wears them all the time, cut back slowly rather than take them away.
- **The listening programs sold to parents do not work.** These go by names like auditory integration training, Berard, and various branded "sound therapies". Usually the child has a set number of sessions listening to altered music through headphones, often at a high cost. A Cochrane review, the most careful kind of research review there is, looked at seven studies. Only two reported a benefit, and those two covered 35 children between them. The reviewers concluded there is no evidence to support these programs. They said the programs should be treated as experimental, that there is a real risk to hearing if they are done badly, and that parents should think hard about the cost. A 2025 review of sound-based approaches was gentler but reached a similar place. It saw some early hints of benefit, but the studies were too few and too different to trust yet.
- **Slow, gentle practice at the child's own pace has the best track record.** The approach with the most evidence behind it is building up tolerance bit by bit. But the way it is done matters enormously. In the therapy version, the child understands the plan, agrees to it, controls the pace, and can stop at any point. That control is not a nicety. It is the part that works. A child who chooses to step toward a sound learns "I can handle this." A child who has a sound forced on them learns something much worse. This approach is well established for sound problems in general. For autistic children specifically, the published evidence is thin: one detailed case of an autistic child who improved and needed his headphones far less afterward.
- **Changing the environment does most of the day-to-day work.** The National Autistic Society treats sensory adjustments at school, meaning changes that make sounds and other sensations easier to bear, as something a child is entitled to and not a favor. The practical list is short and plain. Warn the child before fire drills. Let them wear ear defenders for bells and assemblies. Let them arrive or leave a few minutes early to miss the rush in the hallways. Seat them away from speakers and noisy classmates. Let them take exams in a quiet room. Give them somewhere calm they can go without having to ask. Put felt pads under chair legs. These are the changes that ease daily misery while anything else is going on.

## What this means for caregivers

- **Spend two weeks working out which kind of sound problem your child has.** Keep a note on your phone: which sound, where, and what happened. Was it hands over ears and real distress at something loud? Anger at one specific noise, usually made by a person? Or refusing to go somewhere in case a sound happens? That one difference is what tells a professional which approach to try. You are the only one who can collect it.
- **Ask for a hearing test.** Ask your family doctor (GP) or pediatrician for a referral to audiology, the hearing clinic. It is the first step in every set of guidance. It rules out simple physical causes, and it gives you a starting point to measure against.
- **Treat ear defenders like an umbrella, not a coat.** They are right for fireworks, assemblies, the shopping center, a fire drill. If your child has them on most of the day now, do not just take them away. Cut back slowly, ideally with help from an audiologist or occupational therapist. Replace the covered ears with truly quieter surroundings and planned quiet breaks.
- **Be skeptical of anything sold as a listening program.** Someone may offer a fixed course of sessions listening through headphones to retrain your child's hearing. The evidence says it does not work, and the Cochrane reviewers specifically flagged the cost to families. That money goes further on a hearing appointment or a few therapy sessions.
- **Eleven is a good age for the talking-and-practice approach.** Building up tolerance works far better when a child can understand the plan, agree to it, say how uncomfortable something feels, and stop when they need to. Most 11-year-olds can do that. Look for a psychologist or audiologist who has worked with both autism and sound sensitivity. Expect them to adapt things: pictures instead of talking, small steps the child controls, and calming strategies built in alongside.
- **Get the school adjustments written down.** Advance warning of alarms, permission to leave for a quiet space, seating away from noise, exams in a separate room. Put them in the support plan so they survive a change of teacher.
- **Do not ignore the worry underneath.** By this age, a lot of the trouble comes from dreading a sound rather than hearing one. Avoiding places shrinks a child's world fast. That anxiety can be treated in its own right, and treating it is often what makes everything else possible.

## Important caveats

- This is a summary of research, not a plan for your child. Some signs need a doctor rather than a sensory strategy: sensitivity that is new, in one ear only, or painful, or that comes with ringing or any change in hearing.
- There is no medicine with good evidence for sound sensitivity in autistic children. Medicine is sometimes used for anxiety that has grown around it. That is a different thing, and a decision for a doctor who knows your child.
- Be honest with yourself about how thin the evidence is. The strongest single finding here is a negative one: listening programs do not work. Most of the positive advice comes from what experienced clinicians do, small studies, and single cases. It does not come from large trials.
- The warning about ear defenders comes from clinical experience rather than trials. It has to be weighed against the real freedom they give some children. Nobody is recommending taking them away suddenly, or refusing them somewhere truly deafening.
- A child who has gone quiet and still is not always a child who is coping. Sometimes that is a shutdown, when an overwhelmed child withdraws and stops responding. It can look like progress, and it is not. Going along with something is not the same as tolerating it.
- Sound sensitivity moves around. What your child manages in a quiet shop at 8am may be impossible at 5pm on a Friday. That is the condition, not a sign you are doing it wrong.

## About this research

This summary is built from several sources rather than one study, because no single study covers the question. The strongest piece is a Cochrane review — the most rigorous kind there is — which found no evidence for listening programs. Around it sit a 2025 review of sound-based approaches in children, research establishing how common sound sensitivity is in autism and what forms it takes, small studies of ear defenders and noise-canceling earbuds, and practical guidance from NHS audiology services and the National Autistic Society. Be aware the quality is uneven: the Cochrane finding is solid, but no large trial has tested a full approach specifically in autistic children, so several of the practical recommendations here rest on clinical experience rather than hard proof.

**The research in its own terms.** The findings above are written in everyday words. Here are the terms the papers use, so you can match them:

- **Headphone study (2019):** measured physiological responses in children wearing noise-attenuating headphones, using wearable sensors.
- **Listening programs:** the sessions use processed music delivered through headphones.
