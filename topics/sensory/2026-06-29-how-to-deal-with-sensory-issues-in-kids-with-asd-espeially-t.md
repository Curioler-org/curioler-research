# Understanding Sensory Issues and Humming in Autistic Children: What Caregivers Need to Know

**Source:** Patil O, Kaple M. Sensory Processing Differences in Individuals With Autism Spectrum Disorder: A Narrative Review of Underlying Mechanisms and Sensory-Based Interventions. *Cureus.* 2023; PMC10687592. (Supplemented by clinical guidance from Hopebridge Autism Therapy Centers, Autism Parenting Magazine, Rise Up For Autism, and New Patterns ABA.)
**Evidence level:** Tier 2 — Peer-Reviewed Study
**Domain:** Sensory

## What this research found

- **Differences in how the senses work are very common in autism.** Between 53% and 95% of autistic children show some difference in sensory processing, meaning how the brain takes in what the senses pick up. Their brains may react more strongly, or less strongly, to everyday sights, sounds, textures, smells, and more.

- **Humming is a form of "vocal stimming", and it serves a real purpose.** Stimming means repeated movements or sounds that help a person manage how they feel. Children who hum over and over are often using the sound to self-regulate, or steady themselves. They may be calming down when overwhelmed, blocking out uncomfortable sounds or sensations, keeping focus, or showing excitement or other feelings. It is not misbehavior. It is a coping tool.

- **The brain science behind it is real.** Research shows that autistic people's brains filter and adjust what comes in from the senses differently. The filtering is called sensory gating. It is how the brain screens out signals it does not need. So sounds, textures and other input can feel far stronger than they would for a neurotypical person, meaning someone who is not autistic. That is why soothing habits like humming truly help.

- **Trying to stop or hold back stimming can cause harm.** Several sources agree that suddenly stopping vocal stimming can increase distress, hurt self-esteem, and reduce a child's sense of control. The goal should be understanding and gentle shaping, not getting rid of it.

- **Sensory-based therapies show promise.** One is Sensory Integration Therapy (SIT). It is given by occupational therapists, who help children with everyday skills. Another is ABA therapy (applied behavior analysis, a structured way of teaching skills step by step). Others are changes to the surroundings, like quieter spaces and noise-reducing headphones, and sensory tools, like fidget toys or weighted items. All have shown benefit in helping children cope with sensory challenges more comfortably.

## What this means for caregivers of autistic children

- **Try to see the humming as communication.** When your child hums, especially at meals, when moving from one activity to the next, or in busy places, it is likely their nervous system's way of coping. Notice *when* it increases. That can help you spot what might be overwhelming them, so you can make changes before things build up.

- **Create space for it, and plan around it.** Rather than trying to stop humming entirely, think about when and where it is fine to let it happen freely. A quiet corner at home, noise-reducing headphones in busy places, or a gentle heads-up before a noisy situation can reduce the need for it in the first place.

- **Work *with* therapists, not around them.** Occupational therapists and speech-language pathologists (speech therapists) can carry out a sensory profile assessment. This is a check of exactly what sets your child off. They can build a plan made for your child, which might include activities full of sensations, other outlets for the need, and ways to communicate. The aim is to help your child manage their sensory world more comfortably.

- **Be patient with yourself too.** If your child's humming is constant and you find it hard to tune out, that is completely understandable. Knowing your own sensory limits and finding moments to recharge is part of being able to keep caring for the long run.

## Important caveats

- **This review is a narrative review, not a systematic one.** That means the authors chose which studies to discuss. They did not search all the evidence in a full, planned way that guards against bias. The findings are useful, but read them with some caution. This is Tier 2 evidence, not the highest level available.

- **Every child is different.** Sensory profiles, the mix of things each child is sensitive to or seeks out, vary enormously between autistic children. What works brilliantly for one child (weighted blankets, background music, sensory bins) may not work for another. There is no one-size-fits-all approach.

- **If humming or other sensory behaviors are seriously disrupting your child's daily life, learning, or safety, please seek a professional assessment.** An occupational therapist, pediatrician, or ABA specialist can help find what is driving the behavior and tailor support to it. This summary is not a substitute for that personal guidance.

## About this research

The primary source is a narrative review published in the peer-reviewed journal *Cureus* (2023), in which researchers searched PubMed and the Cochrane Library for studies on sensory processing differences in autism, covering literature from 1999 to 2023. The review examined the neurological mechanisms behind sensory processing differences — including altered brain connectivity and sensory gating dysfunction — as well as the effectiveness of sensory-based therapies. It was supplemented here by clinical practitioner guidance and expert commentary from Hopebridge Autism Therapy Centers, Autism Parenting Magazine, Rise Up For Autism, and New Patterns ABA, which provided applied context on vocal stimming and caregiver strategies.

**The research in its own terms.** The findings above are written in everyday words. Here are the terms the sources use, so you can match them:

- **Narrative review:** the authors selected studies to discuss rather than conducting a comprehensive, bias-controlled analysis of all available evidence.
- **Sensory gating:** autistic individuals differ in how their brains filter and modulate incoming sensory information.
- **Transitions:** humming often increases during meals, transitions and busy environments.
