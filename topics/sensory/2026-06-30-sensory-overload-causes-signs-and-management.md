## At a glance

**Key outcome:** Sensory integration therapy (ASI) meets criteria for evidence-based practice; strong evidence for individual goal achievement, moderate evidence for reducing autistic behaviours and improving self-care  
**Methodology:** Multiple systematic reviews of RCTs and controlled studies; 2025 narrative review of neuroscience literature; observational studies of sensory subtypes  
**When it was added to DSM-5:** 2013 — sensory dysregulation is now a recognised diagnostic criterion for autism  
**Institutes:** American Journal of Occupational Therapy; MDPI Children; PMC / NCBI; Frontiers in Integrative Neuroscience; Nature Translational Psychiatry; Wiley Sensory Neuroscience  

**Limitations:**

- Sensory interventions studied vary widely in method, making it hard to directly compare findings across trials
- Most research is conducted in clinical settings — less is known about what works at home and school
- Evidence for sensory tools like weighted vests and sensory diets is promising but not yet conclusive
- Individual variation is high — what helps one child may not help another

## What this research found

- **Sensory overload is not a behaviour problem — it is a neurological one.** Research shows that autistic brains process sensory input differently at a fundamental level: signals from the eyes, ears, skin, and body are amplified, poorly filtered, or inconsistently integrated. The brain becomes overwhelmed before the child has any choice in the matter.

- **It affects the vast majority of autistic children.** Studies suggest 69–96% of autistic individuals experience some form of sensory dysregulation — far higher than in non-autistic populations. It was officially added to the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for autism in 2013 after years of clinical evidence.

- **There are two main sensory response patterns.** *Hyper-responsivity* (over-sensitivity) means the brain reacts too strongly — a seam in a sock can feel like sandpaper, a crowded room like an assault. *Hypo-responsivity* (under-sensitivity) means the brain under-registers input — a child may seek intense pressure or movement to feel regulated. Many children show both patterns in different sensory channels at different times.

- **Overload builds before it erupts — and has two possible endings.** A *meltdown* is an involuntary release of overwhelm: crying, screaming, pacing, or physical outbursts. A *shutdown* is the opposite — the child becomes unresponsive, stops speaking, and withdraws inward. Both are the nervous system's emergency response to being overwhelmed, not defiance or manipulation.

- **Sensory integration therapy has the strongest evidence base.** Multiple systematic reviews conclude that Ayres Sensory Integration (ASI) — a structured occupational therapy approach — meets criteria as an evidence-based practice. It shows strong outcomes for individual therapy goals and moderate evidence for reducing broader autism-related difficulties and improving self-care. Environmental modifications and sensory tools (headphones, weighted blankets, fidget items) have supporting but less rigorous evidence.

## What this means for caregivers

- **Learn your child's sensory profile before trying to manage overload.** Every child has a unique combination of triggers and sensitivities. Keeping a short diary — noting when distress occurs, what was in the environment, and what preceded it — is one of the most effective things a caregiver can do. Patterns emerge within 1–2 weeks.

- **Overload builds in stages — intervening early is far more effective than responding to a meltdown.** Watch for early warning signs: covering ears, pulling at clothing, squinting, going quiet, or becoming irritable in a specific environment. Removing the trigger or moving to a calm space during this phase can often prevent full overload.

- **A sensory safe space at home makes a real difference.** Designate one quiet area with low lighting, soft textures, and items your child finds regulating — a weighted blanket, noise-cancelling headphones, a favourite sensory toy. This gives your child somewhere to recover without stimulation adding to an already overwhelmed system.

- **During a meltdown or shutdown: safety first, talking later.** Lower your own voice, reduce stimulation, stay close but avoid physical contact unless your child seeks it. Don't try to reason, explain, or address behaviour during the episode — the brain is not in a state to process language. Wait for calm, then reconnect gently.

## Important caveats

- **Occupational therapy referral matters.** Sensory integration therapy should ideally be delivered by an occupational therapist trained in the Ayres approach — the research evidence is specifically for this structured method, not general sensory activities at home or school.

- **Sensory tools are supports, not treatments.** Weighted blankets, noise-cancelling headphones, and fidget items can provide real relief in the moment, but the evidence for them as standalone long-term interventions is limited. They work best as part of a broader sensory strategy.

- **Meltdowns and shutdowns are not the same and may need different responses.** Most guidance focuses on meltdowns, but shutdowns — where a child goes very still or unresponsive — are equally distressing and easily misread as compliance or calm. Knowing which your child experiences matters.

## About this research

This summary draws on several systematic reviews, the most recent being a 2024 MDPI systematic review of sensory processing and sensory integration approaches for autistic children (PMC11506176) and a 2022 AJOT systematic review on sensory interventions and self-regulation. These are complemented by a 2025 Wiley narrative review of sensory neuroscience in autism (Kaundinya et al.), a 2025 Nature Translational Psychiatry paper on in vitro modelling of sensory abnormalities, and a 2025 Frontiers in Integrative Neuroscience piece on sensory-movement underpinnings of neurodivergence. Clinical guidance on meltdown and shutdown recognition draws from NHS Leicestershire Partnership Trust and Reframing Autism.
