## At a glance

**Key outcome:** Sensory integration therapy (ASI) meets criteria for evidence-based practice; strong evidence for individual goal achievement, moderate evidence for reducing autistic behaviors and improving self-care  
**Methodology:** Multiple systematic reviews of RCTs and controlled studies; 2025 narrative review of neuroscience literature; observational studies of sensory subtypes  
**When it was added to DSM-5:** 2013 — sensory dysregulation is now a recognized diagnostic criterion for autism  
**Institutes:** American Journal of Occupational Therapy; MDPI Children; PMC / NCBI; Frontiers in Integrative Neuroscience; Nature Translational Psychiatry; Wiley Sensory Neuroscience  

**Limitations:**

- Sensory interventions studied vary widely in method, making it hard to directly compare findings across trials
- Most research is conducted in clinical settings — less is known about what works at home and school
- Evidence for sensory tools like weighted vests and sensory diets is promising but not yet conclusive
- Individual variation is high — what helps one child may not help another

## What this research found

- **Sensory overload starts in the brain. It is not a behavior problem.** Research shows that autistic brains handle input from the senses differently at a basic level. Signals from the eyes, ears, skin and body can be amplified, poorly filtered, or unevenly combined. The brain is overwhelmed before the child has any choice in the matter.

- **It affects the vast majority of autistic children.** Studies suggest 69 to 96% of autistic people have some form of sensory dysregulation. That means trouble managing how strongly they react to what they see, hear and feel. It is far higher than in non-autistic people. In 2013, after years of clinical evidence, it was officially added to the DSM-5, the manual doctors use to diagnose autism.

- **There are two main patterns of sensory response.** *Hyper-responsivity* means over-sensitivity: the brain reacts too strongly. A seam in a sock can feel like sandpaper, and a crowded room like an attack. *Hypo-responsivity* means under-sensitivity: the brain barely registers input. A child may seek strong pressure or movement to feel settled. Many children show both patterns, in different senses at different times.

- **Overload builds up before it spills over, and it can end in two ways.** A *meltdown* is a release of overwhelm that the child cannot control: crying, screaming, pacing, or physical outbursts. A *shutdown* is the opposite. The child stops responding, stops speaking, and withdraws inward. Both are the nervous system's emergency response to being overwhelmed. Neither is defiance or manipulation.

- **Sensory integration therapy has the strongest evidence.** Several systematic reviews looked at Ayres Sensory Integration (ASI). A systematic review gathers and weighs all the studies on a question. ASI is a set way of working used by occupational therapists, who help children with everyday skills. The reviews say it meets the standard of an evidence-based practice. That means there is enough good research behind it. It shows strong results for each child's own therapy goals. There is moderate evidence that it eases wider autism-related difficulties. It also helps with self-care, the daily tasks of looking after yourself. Changes to the surroundings and sensory tools (headphones, weighted blankets, fidget items) have some support, but the evidence is less solid.

## What this means for caregivers

- **Learn your child's sensory profile before trying to manage overload.** A sensory profile is your child's own mix of triggers and sensitivities, and every child's is different. Keep a short diary: when distress happens, what was around, and what came just before. This is one of the most effective things a caregiver can do. Patterns show up within 1 to 2 weeks.

- **Overload builds in stages, so stepping in early works far better than responding to a meltdown.** Watch for early warning signs: covering ears, pulling at clothing, squinting, going quiet, or getting irritable in a certain place. Removing the trigger or moving to a calm space at this point can often prevent full overload.

- **A sensory safe space at home makes a real difference.** Set aside one quiet area with low light, soft textures, and things your child finds calming, such as a weighted blanket, noise-canceling headphones or a favorite sensory toy. This gives your child somewhere to recover without more stimulation piling onto a system that is already overwhelmed.

- **During a meltdown or shutdown, safety comes first and talking comes later.** Lower your own voice and cut down what is going on around your child. Stay close, but avoid touching them unless they seek it. Don't try to reason, explain, or deal with behavior during the episode. The brain is not in a state to take in language. Wait for calm, then reconnect gently.

## Important caveats

- **An occupational therapy referral matters.** Sensory integration therapy should ideally be given by an occupational therapist trained in the Ayres approach. The research evidence is for this structured method specifically. It does not cover general sensory activities at home or school.

- **Sensory tools are supports, not treatments.** Weighted blankets, noise-canceling headphones and fidget items can bring real relief in the moment. But the evidence for them as long-term treatments on their own is limited. They work best as part of a wider sensory plan.

- **Meltdowns and shutdowns are not the same, and may need different responses.** Most guidance focuses on meltdowns. But shutdowns, when a child goes very still or stops responding, are just as distressing. They are easily mistaken for going along with things, or for calm. Knowing which one your child has matters.

## About this research

This summary draws on several systematic reviews, the most recent being a 2024 MDPI systematic review of sensory processing and sensory integration approaches for autistic children (PMC11506176) and a 2022 AJOT systematic review on sensory interventions and self-regulation. These are complemented by a 2025 Wiley narrative review of sensory neuroscience in autism (Kaundinya et al.), a 2025 Nature Translational Psychiatry paper on in vitro modeling of sensory abnormalities, and a 2025 Frontiers in Integrative Neuroscience piece on sensory-movement underpinnings of neurodivergence. Clinical guidance on meltdown and shutdown recognition draws from NHS Leicestershire Partnership Trust and Reframing Autism.

**The research in its own terms.** The findings above are written in everyday words. Here are the terms the papers use, so you can match them:

- **Mechanism:** sensory overload is described as a neurological rather than a behavioral problem; sensory signals are amplified, poorly filtered, or inconsistently integrated.
- **Meltdown:** an involuntary release of overwhelm.
- **Shutdown:** easily misread as compliance or calm.
- **Self-care:** ASI showed moderate evidence for improving self-care and for reducing autistic behaviors.
