## At a glance

**Key outcome:** Positive relationship found between sleep deprivation and irregular sleep schedule with increased sensation-seeking and risk behavior tendencies.  
**Methodology:** Peer-reviewed study examining associations between sleep deprivation, irregular sleep patterns, and sensation-seeking behavior tendencies.  
**Researchers:** L. Taghvaee  

## What this research found

- Sleep deprivation and variable sleep schedules are linked to higher sensation-seeking behavior.
- Children who sleep poorly tend to seek more movement, pressure, and intense sensory experiences the following day.
- This increased sensory seeking appears to be the body's way of fighting drowsiness and staying alert when tired.

## What this means for caregivers

- If your child seems unusually restless, fidgety, or movement-focused, poor sleep might be the underlying cause rather than simple misbehavior.
- Improving sleep quality and consistency may reduce daytime restlessness and sensory-seeking behaviors.
- The 'tired but wired' pattern—where children seem both exhausted and unable to calm down—makes sense as a physiological response to poor sleep.

## Important caveats

- The study shows sleep and sensory seeking are related, but the direction of cause-and-effect likely goes both ways—poor sleep causes more seeking, and sensory seeking disrupts sleep.
- Most research on this topic involves college students or children already identified with sensory processing challenges, so findings may not apply equally to all children.
- Other factors strongly influence both sleep and daytime sensory seeking, including autism, ADHD, anxiety, and overall sensory sensitivity—sleep is one piece of a complex puzzle.
- The research doesn't specify which types of sensory-seeking behavior increase most with sleep deprivation.
