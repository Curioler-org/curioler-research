## At a glance

**Key outcome:** Positive relationship demonstrated between sleep deprivation and irregular sleep schedules with increased sensation-seeking and risk behavior tendencies  
**Methodology:** Peer-reviewed research examining the relationship between sleep deprivation, irregular sleep schedules, and sensation-seeking behaviors using behavioral and physiological measures.  
**Researchers:** L Taghvaee  

**Limitations:**

- Study may not capture individual variation in how different children respond to sleep loss

## What this research found

- Clear positive relationship between sleep deprivation and increased sensation-seeking behavior
- Irregular sleep schedules amplify sensory-seeking tendencies even more than single nights of poor sleep
- Sleep-deprived children show heightened drive to seek movement, pressure, touch, and other sensory input
- Poor sleep dysregulates the nervous system, making it harder for the body to regulate sensory processing
- This relationship creates a compounding cycle: poor sleep → increased daytime sensory seeking → difficulty settling → worse sleep

## What this means for caregivers

- Your child's daytime restlessness and constant sensory seeking may be a signal that they're not sleeping well enough, not evidence of defiance or behavioral problems
- Fixing sleep problems could be more effective than trying to manage sensory-seeking behaviors in isolation
- When a child hasn't slept well, their nervous system is already in high-alert mode, making them more reactive and more driven to seek sensory input
- This explains why even 'tough-love' approaches to sensory seeking don't work well—the underlying problem is dysregulation from poor sleep

## Important caveats

- This describes correlation: poor sleep and sensory seeking are linked, but the relationship is bidirectional—sensory sensitivities also make sleep harder
- The connection is strongest in children who already have sensory processing sensitivities or differences
- While poor sleep increases sensory seeking, other factors (overstimulation from the day, anxiety, hunger) can also drive these behaviors
- Not every restless, sensory-seeking child has a primary sleep problem—consider the whole picture
