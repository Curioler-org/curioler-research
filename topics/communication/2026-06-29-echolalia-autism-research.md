# Echolalia in Autism: What the Research Tells Us About Why Children Repeat Words and Phrases

**Source:** McAllister et al. (2025), *Autism Research*; Sutherland et al. (2024), *Current Developmental Disorders Reports*; Xie, Pascual & Oakley (2023), *Frontiers in Psychology*
**Evidence level:** Tier 2 — Peer-reviewed study
**Domain:** Communication

## What this research found

- **Echolalia, meaning repeating words or phrases a child has heard, is very common in autistic children.** The largest study reviewed looked at 2,555 autistic children and young people aged 4–17. Parents said around 90% of their children had shown echolalia at some point as they grew up. That makes it one of the most common features of autism.

- **Echolalia is not simply "meaningless repetition."** Research increasingly shows that when an autistic child repeats a phrase, right away or hours later, they are often trying to communicate something. Studies have found that children use echolalia to name things, describe what is happening, keep a conversation going, or even quietly "think out loud" while they look for an answer.

- **How much echolalia a child uses is linked to their language level.** Older children with stronger spoken language tended to use echolalia less over time. But older children and teenagers with more limited spoken language kept using it often. That suggests they were relying on it as a useful way to communicate, rather than growing out of it.

- **Echolalia seems to do two jobs at once.** Research supports the view that it can be a way to communicate *and* a kind of repeated behavior that the child finds calming or enjoyable. Both are valid, and the two do not conflict.

- **Repeated phrases come from many places.** Children may echo something said moments ago (immediate echolalia). Or they may repeat phrases from days or weeks earlier, picked up from caregivers, cartoons, songs or everyday routines (delayed echolalia). Often the echoed phrase means something connected to the moment, even if a listener can't see the link right away.

## What this means for caregivers of autistic children

- **When your child repeats phrases, try to listen for the meaning behind them.** A child saying "time to go to the shops!" when they see a bag, or quoting a line from Peppa Pig when they want a bowl of food, may be using the only words available to them in that moment to express something real. Asking yourself "what might they be trying to tell me?" can open up communication rather than shutting it down.

- **Echolalia is likely a stepping stone, not a dead end.** Research suggests it is often the best way a child has to use language right now. For many children, it is a bridge toward more flexible speech over time. Seeing it as an attempt to communicate, rather than a behavior to stop, may better support your child's development.

- **Pay attention to the context.** Immediate echoing (repeating what you just said) often means your child is engaged and trying to take in what you said or answer it. Delayed echoing (repeating something from earlier or from a show) may carry personal meaning tied to their experiences. Parents often understand these links in ways that professionals, who only see the child briefly, may miss.

- **Working with a speech-language pathologist** (a speech therapist) who understands how children use echolalia to communicate can help you and your child build on what they already do, rather than working against it.

## Important caveats

- **Estimates of how common echolalia is vary widely between studies,** from as low as 25% to as high as 91%. It depends on how each study defined and measured echolalia. There is still no agreed definition and no standard best way to assess it. That makes it hard to say for sure how common it is or how it changes over time.

- **Most research has been done with small groups, or groups that do not reflect all children.** The largest study (McAllister et al., 2025) was mostly boys, mostly White, and mostly from higher-income homes. That limits how widely the findings apply. Girls, non-binary children, and children from different cultures and language backgrounds may have quite different experiences.

- **The research reviewed here does not tell us which approaches are best for supporting a child who uses echolalia.** A related review of the research found that existing studies of therapies are generally of poor quality. They rarely take into account that echolalia can be a way to communicate. Any decisions about speech and language support should be made together with a qualified professional who knows your child.

## About this research

The strongest source summarized here is McAllister et al. (2025), a large peer-reviewed study drawing on data from 2,555 autistic children aged 4–17 in the Simons Simplex Collection — a well-validated, multisite US dataset. Echolalia was captured across nine items from five standardized tools, reported by parents, teachers, and clinicians. This was complemented by Sutherland et al. (2024), a rapid review of 18 studies examining how echolalia prevalence is defined and measured, and by Xie, Pascual & Oakley (2023), a peer-reviewed qualitative and quantitative study of eight Mandarin-speaking preschool children with autism exploring how echolalia functions in everyday speech.

**The research in its own terms.** The sections above use plain words for a few of the papers' terms:

- **Prevalence:** reported prevalence ranges from 25% to 91% depending on definition and measurement; there is no agreed-upon definition or gold-standard assessment.
- **Samples:** most research uses small or unrepresentative samples; the McAllister et al. sample was predominantly male, White and from higher-income households.
- **Interventions:** a related systematic review found existing intervention studies generally of poor quality, rarely accounting for the communicative value of echolalia.
- **Function:** echolalia as both communication and a repetitive behavior the child finds regulating or enjoyable.
