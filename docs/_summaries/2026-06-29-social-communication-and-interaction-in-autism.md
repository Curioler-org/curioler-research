# Social Communication and Interaction in Autism

**Source:** National Autistic Society (autism.org.uk); SCADD Lab, University of Nebraska–Lincoln; University of Kansas School of Education; Autism Research Institute (Dr. Matthew Lerner); Indiana Resource Center for Autism, Indiana University
**Evidence level:** Tier 3 — Clinical consensus and expert guidance drawing on peer-reviewed research
**Domain:** Social

## What this research found

- **Autistic communication is different, not broken.** Research increasingly suggests that autistic people have their own ways of communicating, and their own preferences, rather than simply "lacking" skills. Studies show autistic people often communicate very well with one another. Dr. Damian Milton calls this the "double empathy problem". It means that communication trouble comes from a mismatch between autistic and non-autistic styles, not from a gap on one side only.

- **Social communication varies enormously from person to person.** Some autistic children start talking at the usual age, or even early. Others start later, or may use few or no spoken words all their lives. Many autistic people also find speaking hard at times, for example when overwhelmed by too much noise, light or activity, even if they usually speak easily.

- **Body language, social signals and turn-taking can be hard.** Autistic children may find it harder to use or understand gestures, facial expressions, eye contact and tone of voice. A back-and-forth conversation can take real effort and practice. That includes staying on topic, taking turns, and understanding sarcasm or figures of speech.

- **The senses play a big role in social situations.** Research suggests around 94% of autistic adults experience sensory processing differences, meaning their brains take in sound, light, touch and other senses differently. When a child is overwhelmed by noise, light or crowding, it is much harder to read social signals and feel comfortable with others. Being very sensitive to these things is also strongly linked to social anxiety, which is worry and fear in social situations.

- **Being able to adapt to different social situations may matter more than any single social "skill".** Recent research by Dr. Matthew Lerner suggests two things may predict how well a child gets on socially. One is flexible social thinking. The other is how well a child can read their own emotions. These may matter more than how much the child knows about social rules, or how strongly autism affects them overall.

## What this means for caregivers of autistic children

- **Follow your child's lead on communication.** Speech is not the only way to communicate meaningfully. Eye contact, gestures, sounds, pictures, and Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) devices are all real and valuable. AAC means tools such as picture boards or devices that speak for the child. If your child uses few or no spoken words, trying AAC tools with a speech and language therapist can open up new ways for them to be heard and understood.

- **Small changes in how you interact can make a big difference.** Slow down your own speech. Give your child plenty of time to take in what you said before you expect an answer. Be direct and clear rather than hinting. Cut down on noise, light and other things that can overwhelm them at home. All of this can help your child engage more comfortably. Research supports an important shift here: non-autistic people adapting their style, rather than expecting the autistic child to do all the adapting.

- **Your child almost certainly wants to connect.** A common and harmful myth is that autistic people don't want friendships or relationships. Research and autistic people themselves are clear that the wish to connect is often very much there. What can be exhausting and confusing are the unspoken rules of social life among non-autistic people. Helping your child find communities where they feel naturally understood, including other autistic people, can help a great deal.

- **Look for support that fits your child, not one-size-fits-all.** Several approaches backed by evidence have shown value. They include naturalistic communication approaches (building communication into everyday play and routines), social narratives (short stories that explain a social situation), video modeling (watching a video of someone doing a skill, then copying it), programs where other children are coached to include your child, and PECS (the Picture Exchange Communication System, where a child hands over a picture to ask for something). The right fit depends on your child's own strengths, needs and interests. A speech and language therapist can help find the best place to start.

## Important caveats

- **Most sources here are expert advice and what specialists broadly agree on, not controlled trials.** In a controlled trial, children are put into groups by chance and compared, which is the strongest kind of test. The National Autistic Society (NAS) advice draws on a wide range of published, expert-checked research. But the evidence overall, especially for ways of supporting communication, is still growing. What works for one child may not work for another, so assessing each child on their own is essential.

- **Avoid approaches that mainly aim to make your child look less autistic.** Some therapies train autistic children to mask, meaning hide or hold back, their natural way of communicating, such as by forcing eye contact. These can cause a lot of stress and are linked to poorer mental health. If you are looking into a therapy, ask whether it is led by your child's own goals and comfort.

- **Always involve qualified professionals for assessment and support planning.** A speech and language therapist (SALT) can do a detailed assessment of how your child communicates at any point. In the UK, NICE guidelines (from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence) recommend a team of professionals from different specialties for autism assessment. If you have concerns about your child's communication or well-being, these are the right people to start with.

## About this research

This summary draws on guidance and educational resources from five organizations — the UK's National Autistic Society, the University of Nebraska's SCADD Lab, the University of Kansas School of Education, the Autism Research Institute, and Indiana University's Indiana Resource Center for Autism. The NAS page in particular synthesizes a substantial body of peer-reviewed studies (covering gestures, joint attention, empathy, prosody, language development, and intervention approaches) alongside lived experience from autistic adults and parents. The Autism Research Institute material is based on a recorded academic presentation by Dr. Matthew Lerner (Stony Brook University), reviewing research on mechanisms underlying social development. Together these sources represent current clinical and research consensus rather than a single systematic review.

**The research in its own terms.** The sections above use plain words for a few of the sources' terms:

- **Evidence:** most sources are expert guidance and clinical consensus rather than randomized controlled trials; the NAS guidance draws on peer-reviewed research.
- **Masking:** therapies focused on making a child appear more "neurotypical", including forcing eye contact, are associated with poorer mental health outcomes.
- **Practical terms:** allowing processing time before expecting a response; NICE guidelines (UK) recommend a multidisciplinary team for autism assessment; navigating the unspoken rules of neurotypical social life.
