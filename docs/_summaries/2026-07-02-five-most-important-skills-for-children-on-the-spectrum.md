## At a glance

**Key outcome:** Interventions targeting communication, social skills, and emotional regulation show moderate-to-large effect sizes; play-based and caregiver-mediated approaches consistently amplify outcomes  
**Methodology:** Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses; scoping reviews of functional ability predictors; 2025–2026 reviews of game-based and emotion regulation interventions  
**Key finding:** Language and adaptive skills in early childhood are the strongest predictors of long-term social, behavioural, and independence outcomes  
**Institutes:** PMC / NCBI; Frontiers in Pediatrics; SAGE Journals; Springer Nature; ScienceDirect  

**Limitations:**

- No single study ranks all five skills against each other — this summary synthesises across separate bodies of evidence
- Effect sizes vary considerably by age, support level, and how each skill is measured
- Most research focuses on younger children; evidence for adolescents is thinner
- Skill development is highly individual — priorities differ for every child

## The five skills — and why they matter

### 1. Functional communication

**What it is:** The ability to express needs, thoughts, and feelings in any consistent way — spoken words, signs, picture exchange (PECS), AAC devices, or a combination.

**Why it's foundational:** Communication underpins every other skill on this list. A 2025 meta-analysis of Functional Communication Training (FCT) in natural settings found it significantly reduces challenging behaviour while increasing communicative acts. Research also shows pragmatic language — knowing how and when to use communication socially — is the strongest predictor of adaptive and behavioural outcomes in later childhood and adolescence.

**What helps:** FCT, PECS, AAC introduction, ESDM (Early Start Denver Model), and any approach that treats communication as a need to be met rather than a behaviour to be shaped.

---

### 2. Social skills

**What they are:** The ability to initiate and respond in interactions with others — making eye contact, taking turns, reading social cues, understanding the intent behind what people say and do.

**Why they matter:** Social skills deficits are a core diagnostic feature of autism and affect quality of life, friendships, and long-term independence. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found social skills training interventions produce significant improvements in social communication and reciprocity. Group-based programmes in particular showed large effect sizes for social communication and reductions in repetitive behaviour.

**What helps:** Group social skills programmes (e.g., PEERS for older children), JASPER therapy, peer-mediated intervention, naturalistic developmental behavioural interventions (NDBIs).

---

### 3. Emotional regulation

**What it is:** The ability to recognise, understand, and manage emotions — preventing escalation into meltdown or shutdown, and recovering more quickly when overwhelmed.

**Why it matters:** Poor emotional regulation is one of the most common reasons autistic children struggle at school and in family life. A 2026 systematic review of group-based emotion regulation interventions for autistic children found meaningful improvements in socio-emotional competence, with caregiver involvement identified as a key factor in sustaining gains.

**What helps:** Zones of Regulation, cognitive-behavioural approaches adapted for autism, sensory-aware calm-down strategies, and caregiver coaching in co-regulation (regulating together before the child can self-regulate).

---

### 4. Play skills

**What they are:** The ability to engage in purposeful, flexible play — from simple cause-and-effect toys to pretend play and eventually cooperative play with others.

**Why they matter:** Play is how children practise language, social rules, emotional expression, and problem-solving. It is also the primary context in which all other skills develop naturally. A 2025 Frontiers in Pediatrics meta-analysis of game-based interventions found significant improvements across social, communication, emotional regulation, and cognitive outcomes for autistic children and adolescents.

**What helps:** JASPER (Joint Attention, Symbolic Play, Engagement and Regulation), play-based ABA, the PLAY Project (parent-delivered), and simply following the child's lead in play rather than directing it.

---

### 5. Daily living and self-care skills

**What they are:** Practical independence skills — dressing, eating, toileting, personal hygiene, managing a routine, and eventually more complex skills like cooking, transport, and money.

**Why they matter:** A 2025 scoping review on predictors of functional ability in autism found that adaptive daily living skills — not IQ, not symptom severity — are the strongest predictor of adult independence and quality of life. Children who develop these skills earlier have better outcomes across every domain measured. Cognitive ability matters less than how well a child can function day to day.

**What helps:** Occupational therapy, task analysis (breaking skills into small steps), visual schedules, video modelling, and embedding practice into real daily routines rather than separate therapy sessions.

## What this means for caregivers

- **These five skills are not separate — they build on each other.** Communication enables social interaction. Social interaction develops emotional regulation. Regulated children play better. Play builds daily living skills. Prioritising one area often creates positive ripples across the others.

- **Caregiver involvement is not optional — it is an active ingredient.** Across every domain, the research consistently shows better and more durable outcomes when parents and caregivers are trained as partners in skill development, not just observers of therapy.

- **Earlier is better, but it is never too late.** The largest gains come from early intervention (under 5), but significant improvements have been documented in school-age children and adolescents across all five skill areas. A late start is not a lost cause.

- **"Most important" is individual.** These five areas represent where the evidence is strongest and the downstream impact is widest. But for your child, the most important skill may be the one that is creating the most distress right now. Work with your team to prioritise what matters most in your child's daily life.

## Important caveats

- **There is no consensus ranking in the research.** This summary synthesises across multiple separate evidence bases. Different researchers, different therapies, and different diagnostic perspectives prioritise these skills differently.

- **Evidence quality varies across the five areas.** Communication and social skills have the largest and most rigorous evidence base. Emotional regulation and play interventions have a growing but smaller set of high-quality trials. Daily living skills research is comparatively thin despite being strongly predictive of long-term outcomes.

- **"Skill" and "independence" mean different things for different children.** Functional communication may mean full sentences for one child and a consistent AAC system for another. Both count. The goal is meaningful function, not a neurotypical standard.

## About this research

This summary draws on a 2025 PMC scoping review of functional ability predictors in autism (PMC12885451); a 2025 PMC meta-analysis of social skills training interventions (PMC11918677); a 2025 meta-analysis of Functional Communication Training in natural settings (PMC12730082); a 2025 Frontiers in Pediatrics meta-analysis of game-based interventions; a 2026 SAGE systematic review of group emotion regulation interventions for autistic children; and ScienceDirect longitudinal data on language as a predictor of adaptive outcomes.
