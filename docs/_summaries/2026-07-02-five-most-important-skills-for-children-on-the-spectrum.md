## At a glance

**Key outcome:** Interventions targeting communication, social skills, and emotional regulation show moderate-to-large effect sizes; play-based and caregiver-mediated approaches consistently amplify outcomes  
**Methodology:** Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses; scoping reviews of functional ability predictors; 2025–2026 reviews of game-based and emotion regulation interventions  
**Key finding:** Language and adaptive skills in early childhood are the strongest predictors of long-term social, behavioral, and independence outcomes  
**Institutes:** PMC / NCBI; Frontiers in Pediatrics; SAGE Journals; Springer Nature; ScienceDirect  

**Limitations:**

- No single study ranks all five skills against each other — this summary synthesizes across separate bodies of evidence
- Effect sizes vary considerably by age, support level, and how each skill is measured
- Most research focuses on younger children; evidence for adolescents is thinner
- Skill development is highly individual — priorities differ for every child

## What this research found

### 1. Functional communication

**What it is:** Being able to get needs, thoughts and feelings across in a way the child uses reliably. That can be spoken words, signs, handing over pictures (PECS, the Picture Exchange Communication System), AAC devices, or a mix. AAC stands for augmentative and alternative communication: tools such as picture boards or devices that speak for the child.

**Why it's foundational:** Communication supports every other skill on this list. A 2025 review that combined many studies looked at Functional Communication Training (FCT), which teaches a child a simple way to ask for what they need. Used in everyday settings, it clearly reduced challenging behavior and increased the child's attempts to communicate. Research also shows that social use of language, knowing how and when to use words with other people, is the strongest predictor of how well children manage everyday life and behavior later in childhood and in the teen years. Researchers call this pragmatic language.

**What helps:** FCT, PECS, starting AAC, ESDM (the Early Start Denver Model, a play-based program for young children), and any approach that treats communication as a need to be met rather than a behavior to be shaped.

---

### 2. Social skills

**What they are:** Being able to start an exchange with someone and to respond to one. That covers making eye contact, taking turns, reading social signals, and understanding what people mean by what they say and do.

**Why they matter:** Difficulty with social skills is one of the main signs doctors look for when diagnosing autism. It affects quality of life, friendships and independence later on. A 2025 review that combined many studies found that social skills training brought clear gains in social communication and in back-and-forth with others. Programs run in groups showed large gains in social communication, and fewer repeated behaviors.

**What helps:** Group social skills programs (for example PEERS, a group program for older children), JASPER therapy (explained under play skills below), peer-mediated intervention, where other children are coached to include and play with the child, and NDBIs (naturalistic developmental behavioral interventions), which teach skills through everyday play and routines.

---

### 3. Emotional regulation

**What it is:** Being able to notice, understand and manage feelings. This means stopping a feeling from building into a meltdown or shutdown, and recovering faster when overwhelmed.

**Why it matters:** Trouble managing feelings is one of the most common reasons autistic children struggle at school and at home. A 2026 review looked at group programs that help autistic children manage feelings. Children got meaningfully better at understanding and handling feelings with other people. Parents taking part was a key reason the gains lasted.

**What helps:** Zones of Regulation, a program that uses four colors to help children name how they feel and choose what to do. Also cognitive-behavioral approaches adapted for autism, which help a child notice and change their thoughts and reactions. Calm-down strategies that take account of the child's senses help too, as does coaching parents in co-regulation (calming down together, before the child can calm down alone).

---

### 4. Play skills

**What they are:** Being able to play with purpose and flexibility. That runs from simple cause-and-effect toys, to pretend play, and in time to playing together with others.

**Why they matter:** Play is how children practice language, social rules, showing feelings and solving problems. It is also the main setting where all the other skills grow naturally. A 2025 review in Frontiers in Pediatrics combined studies of game-based programs. It found clear gains for autistic children and teenagers in social skills, communication, managing feelings, and thinking skills.

**What helps:** JASPER (Joint Attention, Symbolic Play, Engagement and Regulation), which builds [joint attention](/curioler-research/summaries/2026-06-29-joint-attention-why-it-matters-in-early-development/), sharing attention on the same thing with someone, and pretend play. Also play-based ABA (applied behavior analysis, which teaches skills in small steps with rewards), the PLAY Project (delivered by parents), and simply following your child's lead in play rather than directing it.

---

### 5. Daily living and self-care skills

**What they are:** Practical skills for doing things on your own: dressing, eating, using the toilet, keeping clean, and following a routine. Later come harder skills like cooking, getting around, and handling money.

**Why they matter:** A 2025 review looked at what predicts how well autistic people manage in daily life. The strongest predictor of independence and quality of life as an adult was everyday living skills. It was not IQ (a score on an intelligence test), and it was not how strongly autism affects the child. Children who build these skills earlier do better in every area that was measured. Thinking ability matters less than how well a child can manage from day to day.

**What helps:** Occupational therapy (help with everyday skills and activities), task analysis (breaking a skill into small steps), visual schedules (a picture plan of the day), video modeling (watching a video of someone doing the skill, then copying it), and building practice into real daily routines rather than separate therapy sessions.

## What this means for caregivers

- **These five skills build on each other.** Communication makes it possible to connect with others. Connecting with others helps a child learn to manage feelings. Children who feel calmer play better. Play builds everyday living skills. Focusing on one area often helps the others too.

- **What you do as a parent is part of what makes support work.** In every area, the research shows better and longer-lasting gains when parents and caregivers are trained as partners in building skills, rather than watching from the side while a therapist works.

- **Earlier is better, but it is never too late.** The biggest gains come from support that starts early (under age 5). But clear improvements have been recorded in school-age children and teenagers in all five skill areas. A late start is not a lost cause.

- **"Most important" depends on your child.** These five areas are where the evidence is strongest and the knock-on effects are widest. But for your child, the most important skill may be the one causing the most distress right now. Work with your child's team to choose what matters most in their daily life.

## Important caveats

- **The research does not agree on a ranking.** This summary pulls together several separate bodies of research. Different researchers, therapies and ways of thinking about diagnosis put these skills in a different order.

- **The strength of the evidence differs across the five areas.** Communication and social skills have the most research, and the most careful. Managing feelings and play have a growing but smaller number of high-quality trials. Research on everyday living skills is fairly thin, even though those skills strongly predict how children do in the long run.

- **"Skill" and "independence" mean different things for different children.** Useful communication may mean full sentences for one child and a reliable AAC system for another. Both count. The goal is communication and skills that work in your child's life, not matching how non-autistic children do things.

## About this research

This summary draws on a 2025 PMC scoping review of functional ability predictors in autism (PMC12885451); a 2025 PMC meta-analysis of social skills training interventions (PMC11918677); a 2025 meta-analysis of Functional Communication Training in natural settings (PMC12730082); a 2025 Frontiers in Pediatrics meta-analysis of game-based interventions; a 2026 SAGE systematic review of group emotion regulation interventions for autistic children; and ScienceDirect longitudinal data on language as a predictor of adaptive outcomes.

**The research in its own terms.** The findings above are written in plain words. Here are the papers' own terms, so you can match them to the sources:

- **Functional communication:** the FCT meta-analysis found it significantly reduces challenging behavior while increasing communicative acts. Pragmatic language is the strongest predictor of adaptive and behavioral outcomes in later childhood and adolescence.
- **Social skills:** social skills deficits are a core diagnostic feature of autism. The 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found significant improvements in social communication and reciprocity; group-based programs showed large effect sizes for social communication and reductions in repetitive behavior.
- **Emotional regulation:** the 2026 systematic review of group-based interventions found meaningful improvements in socio-emotional competence, with caregiver involvement identified as a key factor in sustaining gains.
- **Play:** the game-based meta-analysis found significant improvements across social, communication, emotional regulation and cognitive outcomes in autistic children and adolescents.
- **Daily living:** the scoping review found adaptive daily living skills, not IQ or symptom severity, are the strongest predictor of adult independence and quality of life.
- **Goal:** meaningful function, not a neurotypical standard.
