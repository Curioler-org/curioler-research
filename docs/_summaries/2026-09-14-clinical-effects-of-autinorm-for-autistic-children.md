## At a glance

**Sample size:** Zero participants have been studied on Autinorm. The comparison evidence: a systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 leucovorin studies, including four placebo-controlled trials  
**Demographics:** Autistic children across the leucovorin studies; no Autinorm population exists to describe  
**Key outcome:** No study of Autinorm exists. The review it leans on states that 'd,l-Leucovorin is only form of folate that has been used to treat CFD except for one case report using 5-MTHF' — and 5-MTHF is what Autinorm contains.  
**Methodology:** A product-claim check. The composition of Autinorm was taken from pharmacy listings and the manufacturer's own promotional material, then compared against a 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of cerebral folate deficiency and leucovorin in autism, and against current clinical guidance from the American Academy of Pediatrics and the Child Neurology Society.  
**Researchers:** Daniel A. Rossignol, Richard E. Frye  
**Institutes:** Rossignol Medical Center, Barrow Neurological Institute at Phoenix Children's Hospital, American Academy of Pediatrics, Child Neurology Society  

**Limitations:**

- Absence of published trials is not proof a product does nothing — it means nobody has checked
- The product's own label and recommended daily dosing were read from retail listings, not from a regulatory dossier
- The leucovorin evidence base itself is contested: the Child Neurology Society notes data integrity concerns in several studies
- The meta-analysis authors are the principal investigators of much of the leucovorin research they reviewed
- Individual children with a confirmed diagnosis may respond differently to anything discussed here

## What this research found

- A search for clinical evidence on Autinorm finds no trials, no published studies and no results in autism. It finds only pharmacy listings, social media promotion and talk on parent forums.
- Folate is a B vitamin, and it comes in several forms. Autinorm's active ingredient is Quatrefolic, a form called methylfolate (also written 5-MTHF), at 240 mcg per 5 ml. The autism research it echoes used a different form: leucovorin, also called folinic acid. Leucovorin is a different molecule, and it is available only on prescription.
- That difference is the heart of the matter. Cerebral folate deficiency means too little folate is reaching the brain. It often happens because antibodies, proteins made by the body's own defenses, block the main doorway that carries methylfolate into the brain. Leucovorin is used because it can get in through a second doorway.
- A 2021 review pooled the results of 21 leucovorin studies (a type of review called a meta-analysis). It states directly that folinic acid is the only form of folate used to treat cerebral folate deficiency. The one exception is a single case report, about one patient, that used methylfolate.
- Dose is the other gap. The leucovorin studies gave 2 mg for each kilogram of a child's body weight per day, up to 50 mg a day. Even the group given a "moderate dose" took under 5 mg a day. A 240 mcg serving of Autinorm is 0.24 mg, a fraction of that.
- Even for leucovorin, the Child Neurology Society, a professional group of children's brain and nerve doctors, is cautious. It says no trial has yet been large and careful enough, with a dummy-pill comparison group, to show that leucovorin helps the core signs of autism. It says testing for folate receptor antibodies should not be used as a basis for treatment decisions. And it says leucovorin should not be prescribed as standard care for autism.

## What this means for caregivers

- If Autinorm was recommended to you as evidence-based for autism, the evidence being pointed to is for a different medicine. That medicine has to be prescribed and monitored by a doctor.
- Cerebral folate deficiency is a real diagnosis. It is diagnosed through genetic testing and, for a definite answer, by measuring folate in spinal fluid, the fluid around the brain and spinal cord. It is not diagnosed by trying a syrup and watching.
- If folate has really come up for your child, talk to a pediatric neurologist, a children's doctor who specializes in the brain and nerves, about proper testing. A pharmacy counter is not the place for that conversation.
- The American Academy of Pediatrics notes that reduced folates, a group that includes leucovorin and methylfolate, can shift brain chemistry. The change may be positive, neutral or negative, depending on the child. That is exactly why leucovorin is prescription-only.
- Folate is not automatically harmless at any dose or in any form. The review notes that high doses of plain folic acid, a man-made form of folate, may block that same doorway into the brain. In theory, that could make cerebral folate deficiency worse.

## Important caveats

- Nothing here says Autinorm is dangerous. It says it is untested, and that the research used to sell it studied something else.
- This is a check of the product's claims against published research. It is not a lab test of what is in the bottle.
- The leucovorin field is truly unsettled and still moving. There are promising early findings. There is also serious criticism of how the studies were done, from groups of pediatric neurologists.
- A child who seems better after starting any supplement may be responding to time, therapy, expectation or ordinary development. Telling those apart is exactly what placebo-controlled trials are for. In them, some children get a placebo instead: a dummy that looks the same but does nothing.
- Tell your child's doctor about any over-the-counter product you are giving, including this one, so it can be seen alongside everything else.

## About this research

This summary compares the product's marketed claim against the strongest available evidence for the mechanism it invokes: a 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis in the Journal of Personalized Medicine covering 21 leucovorin studies including four placebo-controlled trials, read alongside current guidance from the American Academy of Pediatrics and a Child Neurology Society position statement. Product composition was taken from pharmacy listings and the manufacturer's own promotional posts. The evidence on leucovorin is real but disputed; the evidence on Autinorm specifically does not exist.

**The research in its own terms.** The findings above are written in plain words. Here are the terms the sources use, so you can match them up:

- **Composition:** Quatrefolic is a methylfolate (5-MTHF) salt; the research used d,l-leucovorin (folinic acid).
- **Mechanism:** cerebral folate deficiency often results from autoantibodies blocking the folate receptor alpha, the transporter that carries 5-MTHF into the brain; leucovorin can enter through the reduced folate carrier. High-dose folic acid may block the folate receptor.
- **Dose:** 2 mg/kg/day up to 50 mg daily, with the "moderate dose" arm below 5 mg/day.
- **Child Neurology Society:** there is no adequately powered, pre-registered placebo-controlled trial establishing efficacy for core autism symptoms; folate receptor antibody testing is not recommended as a basis for clinical decisions; leucovorin should not be prescribed as standard care for autism.
- **The criticism:** serious methodological criticism from pediatric neurology bodies.
