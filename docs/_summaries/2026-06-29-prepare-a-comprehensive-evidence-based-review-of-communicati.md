# Communication in Autism Spectrum Disorder: What the Research Tells Us

**Source:** Multiple peer-reviewed sources including a Cochrane systematic review, a narrative review, two peer-reviewed studies, and a university-based practice guide
**Evidence level:** Tier 1 — Systematic Review / Meta-Analysis (Cochrane review is the strongest source; other sources are Tier 2 peer-reviewed studies and Tier 3/4 practice guidance)
**Domain:** Communication

## What this research found

- **Communication differences are central to autism.** Autistic children show a wide range of communication profiles — from no spoken words at all, to fluent speech with subtle social communication differences. Around 25–30% of autistic children remain minimally verbal (using fewer than 30 functional words) even into school age. Across the spectrum, the social use of language — knowing how to hold a conversation, take turns, understand humour or sarcasm, and adapt language to context — is typically the most affected area, even when formal grammar and vocabulary appear intact.

- **Pragmatic and social communication challenges are the core difficulty.** Research consistently shows that autistic children struggle more with *how* language is used socially (pragmatics) than with the structure of language itself. These difficulties include: understanding non-literal language like jokes or irony, adjusting conversation to the listener's needs, interpreting facial expressions and gestures, and maintaining back-and-forth dialogue. A Brazilian study found autistic children scored significantly lower on social-pragmatic assessments than both typically developing children and children with developmental language disorder.

- **AAC and other communication interventions show promise but evidence is limited for minimally verbal children.** A Cochrane systematic review — the gold standard in evidence — found only two eligible randomised controlled trials (154 children total) studying communication interventions for minimally verbal autistic children. The Picture Exchange Communication System (PECS) increased use of picture symbols and verbal initiations immediately after the intervention, but gains were not maintained ten months later. A play-based parent training programme did not produce significant group-wide expressive language gains, though children with very low baseline language made more progress. The overall evidence quality was rated *very low*.

- **Multiple evidence-based communication strategies are available.** Research-supported approaches include: PECS (picture-based communication), speech-generating devices (SGDs) and AAC apps (such as Proloquo2Go), visual schedules, social stories, Pivotal Response Training, naturalistic language strategies, and peer-mediated interventions. Early intervention — beginning before age five — is consistently associated with better outcomes. AAC does not prevent speech development; evidence suggests it can support it.

- **Language profiles in autism and related conditions overlap, but are distinct.** A literature review of 18 studies comparing autism, Developmental Language Disorder (DLD), and Social (Pragmatic) Communication Disorder found that when children are matched on vocabulary or working memory skills, their language profiles can look very similar — particularly in the area of word knowledge and semantics. However, the social communication difficulties in autism are typically more pronounced than in DLD, and the presence of restricted/repetitive behaviours remains a key distinguishing feature of autism.

## What this means for caregivers of autistic children

- **Early, individualised communication support makes a real difference.** The research is clear that starting communication intervention as early as possible — ideally in the toddler and preschool years — gives children the best chance of building functional language skills. If your child is not yet speaking or has limited speech, asking for a speech-language pathology assessment early is one of the most valuable steps you can take.

- **AAC is not giving up on speech — it can help build it.** Many parents worry that introducing picture systems or speech-generating devices will reduce their child's motivation to speak. The evidence does not support this fear. AAC is designed to *add to* a child's communication, not replace speech. In fact, research suggests it can actually support spoken language development by reducing communication pressure and giving children another way to connect.

- **Your observations matter enormously.** Research confirms that parents and caregivers are excellent reporters of their child's communication abilities. Your daily observations about how your child communicates at home — what they respond to, how they initiate, what frustrates them — are valuable information for therapists and teachers, and should be actively included in planning.

- **Consistency across settings helps.** Communication strategies work best when they are used consistently at home, at school, and in therapy. Collaboration between speech-language pathologists, educators, and family members is highlighted across multiple studies as a key factor in supporting children with complex communication needs.

## Important caveats

- **The evidence base, especially for minimally verbal children, is thin.** The Cochrane review found only two qualifying trials for minimally verbal autistic children, and rated the evidence quality as *very low*. This does not mean interventions don't work — it means we don't yet have the large, rigorous studies needed to be confident about what works best, for whom, and for how long. Many promising studies exist but used different comparison groups or designs.

- **Children's responses to intervention vary widely.** No single communication approach works for every child. Baseline language level, joint attention skills, cognitive ability, and individual learning style all appear to influence which approach is most helpful. What works well for one child may not suit another, even with a similar diagnosis. This is why personalised assessment by a qualified speech-language pathologist is so important.

- **Most studies are small and focused on younger children.** Much of the research reviewed here involved preschool and early school-age children. There is a notable gap in evidence for older children and adolescents who remain minimally verbal. Parents of older children should be aware that less is known about what helps at these ages, but this does not mean intervention is futile.

## About this research

The strongest source underpinning this summary is a 2018 Cochrane systematic review (Brignell et al.) that searched 18 databases and assessed all randomised controlled trials of communication interventions for minimally verbal autistic children. It included two studies involving 154 children aged 32 months to 11 years. Alongside this, a 2022 narrative literature review (Félix et al.) examined 18 comparative studies of language profiles across autism, DLD, and Social Pragmatic Communication Disorder involving nearly 1,500 participants. A peer-reviewed Brazilian study (Hage et al., 2021) assessed social-pragmatic skills in 40 children aged 3–6 using parent and teacher questionnaires. Practice guidance from Indiana University's Resource Center for Autism and a 2024 master's thesis from St. Cloud State University synthesised evidence on communication strategies and AAC tools for early childhood. Taken together, these sources provide a broad picture of the current state of research, from foundational language comparisons to practical intervention options.
