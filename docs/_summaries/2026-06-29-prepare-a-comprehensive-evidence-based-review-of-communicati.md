# Communication in Autism Spectrum Disorder: What the Research Tells Us

**Source:** Brignell et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (2018); supplemented by Theodoratou (2024) and practitioner resources from Indiana University IRCA and LeafWing Center
**Evidence level:** Tier 1 — Systematic Review (primary source), with Tier 2–4 supporting sources
**Domain:** Communication

## What this research found

- **Communication difficulties are common, but they vary a lot.** Around 25–30% of autistic children do not develop spoken language they can use day to day. Some stay "minimally verbal," meaning they use fewer than 30 words. Children who do learn to speak still very often find social communication hard. That includes taking turns in conversation, understanding words not meant literally (like jokes or sayings), and reading the unspoken signals in faces, gestures and tone of voice.

- **No single approach has been shown to work for all children.** A Cochrane review is a careful, independent summary of all the strong trials on one question. One such review looked at communication approaches for minimally verbal autistic children and found only two high-quality trials. One tested the Picture Exchange Communication System (PECS), where a child learns to hand over a picture to ask for something. The other tested a play program carried out at home. Both showed some short-term gains in certain communication skills. Neither produced gains that clearly lasted for most children. The reviewers rated the overall strength of the evidence as *very low*.

- **Some children respond better to certain approaches than others.** How much language a child has at the start seems to matter. In the home play study, children who could say very little at the start made more progress. In other studies, two skills helped predict which children benefited from which kind of therapy. One is joint attention: sharing focus on something with another person, such as both looking at the same toy. The other is imitation, copying what others do.

- **A range of tools and strategies look promising.** Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) means ways to communicate other than speech, such as picture boards, sign language and devices that speak aloud. AAC can help children communicate even when speech is limited, and research suggests it does *not* hold back spoken language. Other approaches with good support include Functional Communication Training (FCT), which teaches a child a simple way to ask for what they need. Pivotal Response Training (PRT) builds skills through play and the child's own interests. Video modeling has a child watch a short video of a skill and then try it. Social skills training is also on the list.

- **When ADHD and autism occur together, communication gets harder.** ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder) affects attention, activity levels and self-control. Research suggests 50–70% of autistic people also have ADHD traits. The mix can make conversation harder. For example, acting on impulse may lead to interrupting, and trouble keeping attention makes a long back-and-forth more difficult.

## What this means for caregivers of autistic children

- **Early support matters, so it is worth seeking it sooner rather than later.** Research consistently suggests that children who develop communication skills before age five tend to do better in the long run. If you are worried about your child's communication, seeing a speech-language pathologist (a speech therapist) early is one of the most helpful steps you can take.

- **There is no one-size-fits-all answer, and that is okay.** Your child is unique, and the right mix of strategies may look different from another family's. AAC, visual supports (pictures and other things a child can see, not just hear), and structured communication practice such as FCT or PECS can all be good starting points. A qualified therapist can help you find what fits best.

- **You are a key part of your child's communication.** In parent-mediated approaches, caregivers learn strategies to use at home. This is a growing and promising area of research. Simple things can help in everyday moments: following your child's lead, using visual schedules (a picture sequence of the day), and giving your child extra time to take in what you said before they respond.

- **Progress may be slow and uneven, and that is normal.** Research shows that communication in autistic children often grows in fits and starts rather than in a straight line, and very differently from child to child. Tracking progress over time with your child's therapy team helps make sure support is adjusted as your child grows.

## Important caveats

- **The strongest evidence is limited.** The Cochrane review, the most careful source available, found only two trials that met its standards for minimally verbal children. Both were small and had weaknesses in how they were designed. This does not mean these approaches do not help. It means we do not yet have strong proof about *which* ones work best, for *whom*, and for *how long*.

- **Many widely used approaches still lack large, high-quality trials.** Some of them have good supporting evidence, but its quality varies. They include ABA (applied behavior analysis), a structured way of teaching skills using rewards. They also include social narratives, which are short stories that explain a social situation, and naturalistic language strategies, which build language during everyday play and routines. Always ask your therapist what research supports the approach they are recommending.

- **This summary cannot replace a professional assessment.** Every autistic child is different. A speech-language pathologist, an autism specialist, or a team of different specialists is best placed to assess how your child communicates. They can then suggest a plan made for your child. If you are concerned about your child's communication, please ask for a professional evaluation.

## About this research

The primary source is a Cochrane systematic review (Brignell et al., 2018), which searched 18 databases and identified two randomized controlled trials (154 children aged 32 months to 11 years) comparing communication interventions to a control group in minimally verbal autistic children. The review followed rigorous Cochrane methodology, including independent screening, risk-of-bias assessment, and GRADE evidence quality ratings. Supporting context was drawn from a 2024 peer-reviewed mini-review on ADHD-ASD communication comorbidity (Theodoratou, 2024), and practitioner guidance from Indiana University's Resource Center for Autism and the LeafWing Center, which synthesize evidence-based practices used in clinical settings.

**The research in its own terms.** The findings above are written in everyday words. Here are the technical terms, so you can match them to the papers:

- **Language:** 25–30% of autistic children do not develop functional spoken language or remain minimally verbal (fewer than 30 words); social communication difficulties include conversational turn-taking, non-literal language and non-verbal cues.
- **Cochrane review (Brignell et al., 2018):** two randomized controlled trials, one of PECS and one of a home-based play program (FPI), each with some short-term gains in certain communication skills but no clearly maintained improvement for most children; GRADE quality of evidence very low; both trials small, with methodological limitations.
- **Predictors of response:** lower baseline expressive language predicted greater progress in the FPI study; joint attention and imitation predicted differential response in other studies.
- **Other practices named:** AAC, Functional Communication Training (FCT), Pivotal Response Training (PRT), video modeling, social skills training, parent-mediated approaches; ABA, social narratives and naturalistic language strategies still lack large-scale RCT evidence.
- **Development:** communication development in autistic children is described as non-linear and highly individual; assessment by a speech-language pathologist, autism specialist or multidisciplinary team.
