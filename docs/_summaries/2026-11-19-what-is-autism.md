## At a glance

**Sample size:** No single participant group. The review draws on many studies, including a surveillance study of over 2,000 autistic children and a study of over 600 autistic children seeking treatment.  
**Demographics:** Mixed: children, adolescents and adults across the studies reviewed, mostly from the United States, with some from Denmark and elsewhere. The review notes that autism occurs in all racial, ethnic and income groups.  
**Key outcome:** Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition defined by differences in social communication plus restricted interests and repetitive behaviors. Many genetic and environmental factors contribute, but no single cause has been identified.  
**Methodology:** A peer-reviewed narrative review (an expert overview, not a systematic search) of published research on autism's definition, prevalence, causes and evaluation, supported by four non-peer-reviewed university and advocacy web pages.  
**Researchers:** Holly Hodges, Casey Fealko, Neelkamal Soares  

## What this research found

- The way doctors define autism changed in 2013. Several older labels, including autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder and PDD-NOS (a label for milder or less typical cases), were merged into one diagnosis called autism spectrum disorder. Sensitivity to sound, texture or light was added as a recognized feature, and levels describing how much support a person needs were introduced. The new criteria seem better at confirming that someone who is diagnosed really has autism, but they may miss some people, especially older children, teens, adults, and those who once had an Asperger's or PDD-NOS label. One systematic review found that only 50% to 75% of people kept their diagnosis under the new criteria, while another study found 91%.
- Genes play a large role. Siblings of autistic children have a higher chance of being autistic, and identical twins are much more likely than other siblings to both be autistic, though not always. Variants in more than 700 genes have been linked to autism, and a few genetic conditions, such as fragile X syndrome, tuberous sclerosis and Down syndrome, raise the chance. Those conditions explain only a very small share of cases. Other factors linked to higher chances include older parents, premature birth, and exposure to certain medicines in pregnancy (valproic acid and thalidomide). Studies on a mother's autoimmune disease or infection during pregnancy have mixed results. The review found no evidence that vaccines, thimerosal or mercury are linked to autism. The largest single study, of a nationwide group of Danish children, found no higher chance after the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine.
- Estimates of how common autism is have risen over time. The review cites about 1 in 59 US 8-year-olds, based on 2014 data. Autism Speaks, an advocacy group, reports a newer CDC estimate of 1 in 31. The review says the rise is likely due partly to more awareness, insurance coverage for services, and changes in how cases are counted. A Danish study found that most of one earlier rise came from changes in criteria and reporting. Boys are diagnosed more often than girls, but a cited meta-analysis puts the true ratio nearer 3 boys to 1 girl than 4 to 1. Girls are more likely to be diagnosed late or missed, partly because some hide, or "camouflage," their social difficulties. White children are diagnosed more often than Black or Hispanic children, though the gap seems to be shrinking.
- Other conditions are common alongside autism. In a surveillance study of over 2,000 autistic children, 83% had another developmental diagnosis, 10% had a mental health diagnosis, and 16% had a nervous system diagnosis. The review cites rates of about 20% for epilepsy, 10% to 12% for stomach and bowel problems, 50% to 73% for sleep problems, and 25% to 81% for ADHD. The most recent CDC estimate is that about 31% have an intellectual disability. In a study of over 600 autistic children seeking treatment, 42% had an anxiety disorder, and about two-thirds had more than one additional condition.

## What this means for caregivers

- Autism is not one single profile. Children differ widely in speech, in how much support they need, and in their strengths. Having some of the behaviors described does not by itself mean a child is autistic. Only a specialist evaluation can make that call.
- Pediatric guidelines described in the review call for developmental check-ins at 9, 15 and 30 months and autism-specific screening at 18 months and again at 24 or 30 months. If screening raises concerns, the next steps are early intervention services for children under 3, or a school evaluation for children 3 and older, plus referral to a specialist for a full diagnosis. Early signs the review lists include little response to their name, few gestures by 12 months, and loss of language or social skills. Your child's doctor can tell you which of these apply to your family.
- Because other health needs are common, a full evaluation often looks at sleep, digestion, seizures and anxiety too. The review says several professional groups recommend genetic testing as part of the first medical workup. A page from a university autism center says no medicine treats autism itself, though medicines can help specific problems like seizures. The same page says treatments such as chelation and hyperbaric oxygen have no scientific support and carry safety risks.

## Important caveats

- The main source is a 2020 expert review, not a systematic search of all studies, so the authors chose which research to include. Some numbers are older. The 1 in 59 figure comes from 2014 data, and the newer 1 in 31 figure here comes from an advocacy website that cites the CDC.
- The risk factors listed are links found in studies, not proven causes. A meta-analysis the review cites found such large differences between studies that it could not judge how much each factor matters. The review says that no causal determinations have been made.
- The other four sources are web pages from universities and advocacy groups, not peer-reviewed papers, and their numbers differ. One foundation page says about 40% of autistic people have an intellectual disability and 30% to 50% have seizures, versus 31% and about 20% above. Average diagnosis age is described as about 5 by one source and near 3 by research centers. One university center also reports its own findings, such as early brain overgrowth and a split of autism risk into 30% to 45% common gene variants and 30% to 45% environment. We did not check those findings against published papers.
- There is no blood test or brain scan that diagnoses autism. Diagnosis rests on a specialist's observation and a parent interview, so these findings cannot tell you whether any one child is autistic.
