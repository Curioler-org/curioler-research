## At a glance

**Sample size:** The one proper trial of CBT for sound sensitivity involved 60 adults. A separate trial for trigger-sound problems ran group CBT with around 56% of those who finished showing improvement. For autistic children specifically, the published evidence is one case — a single child  
**Demographics:** The trial evidence is almost entirely adults. Separately, CBT for anxiety in autistic children has been tested properly — pooled analyses cover 19 trials and 833 children — but that tells us CBT can work with autistic children, not that it works for sound sensitivity  
**Key outcome:** CBT is the best-evidenced treatment for sound sensitivity, and that is a real finding. But the bar it clears is low: it is the only approach with a proper trial behind it, and that trial was in adults. 'Best available' is fair. 'Best' overstates it  
**Methodology:** Pulls together the single randomized trial of CBT for sound sensitivity, trial evidence for CBT with trigger-sound problems, a 2025 review of CBT across sound-intolerance conditions, pooled analyses of CBT for anxiety in autistic children, a published case of an autistic child treated for sound sensitivity, and reviews mapping what is still missing  
**Institutes:** Linköping University, Sweden, Amsterdam UMC, Vanderbilt University Medical Center, NHS audiology services (UK)  

**Limitations:**

- Not one trial has tested CBT for sound sensitivity in autistic people — the entire autism-specific evidence is a single published case
- The main trial was in adults, and an 11-year-old is not a small adult
- Nothing has compared CBT head-to-head against sound therapy, occupational therapy or simply changing the environment, so 'better than the alternatives' has never actually been tested
- The trial compared CBT against a waiting list, not against another active treatment — some of the benefit may come from getting attention and support rather than from CBT specifically
- How well CBT works for an autistic child depends a lot on the child, and the evidence thins out considerably for children with significant intellectual disability

## What this research found

- **There is one proper trial, and CBT passed it.** CBT (cognitive behavioral therapy) works on how a person thinks about something hard and how they respond to it. In the trial, 60 adults with sound sensitivity were split at random into two groups. One group had CBT. The other went on a waiting list, so they had no treatment yet. The CBT group did better on nearly every measure, and the gain was still there a year later. What improved matters too. People felt less distressed. And the point at which sound became uncomfortable for them, measured in testing, moved as well. The effect was moderate: real, but not a cure.
- **The same approach also helps with trigger sounds.** Some people are bothered less by loudness and more by specific sounds, such as chewing, sniffing or tapping. In a trial of group CBT for this kind of problem, around 56% of those who finished it improved. A further trial has since been run with children and teenagers aged 8 to 18, mixing CBT with movement-based therapy. So the approach seems to carry across different kinds of sound sensitivity, which is a point in its favor.
- **But there is a gap exactly where autistic children should be.** No trial has ever tested CBT for sound sensitivity in autistic people. Not a small one, not a weak one: none. All the autism-specific evidence is one published case. It describes a single autistic child who improved and needed his ear defenders far less afterward. Researchers who review this area say plainly that there is no gold-standard treatment, meaning one proven to work best, for autistic people who struggle with sound.
- **We do know CBT works for autistic children on other problems.** Pooled analyses combine the results of many trials. Those covering 19 trials and 833 autistic children show CBT really does help with anxiety. But look at who did the rating, because it matters. Clinicians, the trained professionals assessing the children, saw a large improvement. Parents saw a modest one. The children themselves reported the smallest change of all. Same treatment, three very different verdicts. Keep that gap in mind: depending on whose report you read, CBT looks either life-changing or mildly useful.
- **It has to be adapted, and that is normal.** The versions that work with autistic children lean much more on doing than on talking about thoughts and feelings. Therapists add visual supports, such as pictures. They drop the need for eye contact, leave out deep inward-looking work on a person's core beliefs, and change how they communicate. This matters in practice. A child who finds it hard to notice and describe their own thinking gets less from the standard version. For children with significant intellectual disability, meaning much greater difficulty with learning and reasoning, the evidence becomes really thin.
- **Nobody has run the comparison that would justify calling it "best".** No study has put CBT up against the other options to see which comes out ahead. Those options include sound therapy, which uses sound itself to build tolerance, and occupational therapy (OT), which helps with everyday skills. They also include simply changing the child's surroundings. Reviewers of sound sensitivity treatments list exactly that comparison as an open priority. So when CBT is called the best treatment, it really means *the only one anyone has properly tested*. That is a different and more modest claim.

## What this means for caregivers

- **If someone tells you CBT is the best therapy for sound sensitivity, they are roughly right, but for a weaker reason than it sounds.** It has the best evidence because it is nearly the only one with real evidence. It was never compared with the other options and found to win. That is still a good reason to take it seriously. It is not a reason to drop everything else.
- **It is a reasonable thing to ask for at 11.** Around this age the approach starts to work well. It depends on the child understanding the plan, agreeing to it, saying how uncomfortable something feels, and being able to stop. Most 11-year-olds can do that with the right support.
- **Ask the therapist what they will actually do.** Look for someone who works at your child's pace and lets your child control how far each step goes. They should adapt the method, not run a standard plan made for adults. Part of the work is exposure: facing difficult sounds a step at a time. If the plan exposes your child to sounds without their agreement, that is not the version that was tested.
- **Don't skip the cheaper, simpler steps to get to therapy.** A hearing check comes first. So does a hard look at your child's surroundings: the noisy classroom, the assembly hall, the hand dryer in the school bathrooms. CBT is a good option, not the first move. It works better when the surroundings are not fighting it.
- **Be careful about what "it's working" means.** Clinicians, parents and children rated the same treatment very differently. So ask your child directly and take their answer seriously, even when a professional is more hopeful. A child who has stopped protesting is not always a child who is more comfortable.
- **Say early on if your child finds it hard to talk about thoughts and feelings.** That is useful information, not a barrier. It tells a good therapist to use the more practical version, which focuses on what your child does. That version generally works better for autistic children anyway.
- **Nothing in this research supports an adult simply forcing exposure.** In the version that was tested, the person facing the sounds was in control of it. That is not a small detail. It seems to be one of the parts that makes the treatment work.

## Important caveats

- This is a research summary, not advice about your child. If sound sensitivity is new, painful or in one ear only, see a doctor first. The same goes if it comes with ringing in the ears or any change in hearing.
- "Has the best evidence" and "will work for your child" are different things. The trial found a moderate average effect. That means some people improved a lot and others barely at all.
- The main trial was in adults. Children are not simply smaller adults. How far these results carry over to an 11-year-old is truly unknown.
- CBT cannot fix surroundings that are truly overwhelming. If a child spends the day somewhere painfully loud, therapy is being asked to do a job that belongs to the environment.
- In many places it is hard to find a therapist who knows both autism and sound sensitivity. Exposure done badly, too fast or without the child's consent, can make things worse and cost you your child's trust.

## About this research

The centerpiece here is a single randomized controlled trial of CBT for sound sensitivity in 60 adults, supported by trial evidence for trigger-sound problems, a 2025 review summarizing CBT across sound-intolerance conditions, and pooled analyses of CBT for anxiety in autistic children. Be clear-eyed about the shape of this evidence: it is strong enough to say CBT is worth trying and weak enough that anyone claiming it is definitively the best option is going beyond what has been shown. The gap that matters most for this question — a trial in autistic children — has not been filled.
