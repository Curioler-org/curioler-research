## At a glance

**Key outcome:** Interventions improve joint attention skills with a moderate effect size (g = 0.53, 95% CI [0.34, 0.72]) across 18 RCTs involving 1,165 children  
**Methodology:** Meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials; systematic reviews of parent-mediated and preschool-based programs; longitudinal eye-tracking studies  
**Typical emergence:** 9–12 months in neurotypical development  
**Researchers:** Mundy, Kasari, Sigman and others across multiple centers  
**Institutes:** Multiple — including UCLA, MIND Institute, and European research consortia  

**Limitations:**

- Substantial heterogeneity across RCTs makes it hard to generalize findings to all children
- Most trials focus on initiating joint attention; gains in language and global social functioning are smaller and less consistent
- Many studies have small samples, short follow-up periods, and limited demographic diversity

## What this research found

- **Joint attention starts in the first year of life.** Joint attention means sharing focus on the same thing with another person. Most babies begin following a caregiver's gaze (where they are looking) or pointing between 9 and 12 months. By 12 months, babies who share attention this way reliably go on to learn words faster. That makes it one of the earliest signs of how language will develop.

- **There are two kinds.** *Responding to joint attention (RJA)* means following someone else's look or point to an object. *Initiating joint attention (IJA)* means drawing someone else's attention to something you find interesting. A child might point, show you something, or look back and forth between you and an object. Autistic children often do less IJA, even when their RJA is fairly typical.

- **Delays in joint attention are among the earliest signs of autism that can be detected.** Some studies use eye-tracking, a camera that records exactly where a child is looking. They show that toddlers with autism look at joint attention cues, such as a point or a glance, differently from other toddlers. One study followed babies over time. At 12 months, how easily a baby could move their attention away from one thing predicted two things at 24 months: their joint attention, and their wider social communication.

- **Early support can improve joint attention.** A 2025 review pooled 18 trials, with 1,165 children in all. In each trial, children were put into groups by chance. Programs aimed at joint attention improved those skills by a moderate amount, more than chance alone would explain. Younger children tended to gain more. A 2025 study also found that early support helps autistic preschoolers respond more when others try to share attention with them. A key part of why it helped seemed to be how well a child could keep their level of alertness and excitement comfortable, which researchers call arousal regulation.

- **Programs led by parents have growing evidence.** In these, parents learn to build joint attention into everyday routines. A 2024 review found gains in both joint attention and language when they did. The studies included 4 trials with groups chosen by chance, plus several careful studies that each tracked one child closely over time.

## What this means for caregivers

- **You are your child's most important joint attention partner.** Everyday moments build the brain pathways for language and social connection. That means following your child's gaze, pointing things out during play, and talking about what they're looking at. You don't need a clinic for this.

- **Look for pointing and following your gaze, not just words.** Notice whether your child points to share interest, not just to ask for things. Notice whether they follow where you look. If they aren't doing these by 12 to 14 months, mention it to your GP (family doctor) or health visitor (a nurse who checks on young children's development). This is not cause for alarm. It is because early support makes a real difference.

- **If your child has already been identified as autistic, joint attention is a worthwhile goal.** The research is encouraging. Structured programs do improve these skills, especially when they happen in everyday settings and caregivers are actively involved. And gains in joint attention often predict gains in language over time.

- **Progress may be slow, and it looks different in every child.** Some children get better at responding to joint attention before they start it themselves. Celebrate both. A child who begins to make eye contact to share a smile, even briefly, is developing joint attention.

## Important caveats

- **Gains in joint attention don't always carry over to language or social skills overall.** The evidence that programs improve joint attention itself is stronger than the evidence that the gains spread to wider communication in everyday life. More research is needed on long-term results.

- **Most research participants are young children, of preschool age.** Less is known about how joint attention develops, and how to support it, in school-age children and teenagers. The same is true for children who also have an intellectual disability, meaning much greater difficulty with learning and reasoning.

- **The research base is still building.** Many studies are small and define joint attention in different ways, so it is hard to compare findings across programs. The field has not yet agreed on one standard way to measure it.

## About this research

The strongest evidence comes from a 2025 meta-analysis published in the *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders* (Springer Nature), which synthesized 18 RCTs involving 1,165 children and found a moderate effect of joint attention interventions. This is supported by a 2024 systematic review in the *Journal of Child and Adolescent Psychiatric Nursing* examining parent-mediated approaches. Longitudinal eye-tracking studies — including a 2025 MDPI study tracking infants at elevated likelihood of autism from 12 to 24 months — add important early-detection context. Foundational developmental data on typical joint attention milestones draws on a broad base of established research reviewed in resources from Child and Family Development and PMC longitudinal studies.

**The research in its own terms.** The findings above are written in plain words. Here are the terms the papers use, so you can match them up:

- **Eye-tracking (2024):** differences in visual attention toward joint attention cues in toddlers with autism compared to typically developing peers.
- **Longitudinal study (2025):** how well an infant disengages visual attention at 12 months predicts joint attention ability and broader social communication at 24 months.
- **Meta-analysis (2025):** a moderate, statistically significant effect of joint attention interventions on joint attention skills (g = 0.53).
- **Preschool study (2025):** early intervention increases reactive joint attention in autistic preschoolers, with arousal regulation identified as a key mediating factor.
- **Parent-mediated review (2024):** designs included 4 RCTs and several well-controlled single-subject studies.
- **Caveats:** less is known about joint attention development and intervention in adolescents and in children with co-occurring intellectual disability; the field has no agreed gold-standard measure.
