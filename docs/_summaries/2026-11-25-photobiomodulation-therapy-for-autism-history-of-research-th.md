## At a glance

**Sample size:** 30 children in the main randomized trial (16 real light, 14 fake light). A 2022 retrospective study included 21 children.  
**Demographics:** Main trial: ages 2–6 (average about 4.8), 24 boys and 6 girls, 22 of 30 White, 12 of 30 nonverbal, mostly moderate-to-severe autism ratings, recruited in New York City. Children on regular medication or with a seizure history were excluded. The 2022 study: ages 5–15 (average 9.1), 13 boys and 8 girls, in Florence, Italy.  
**Key outcome:** Autism-rating scores dropped about 9.9 points with real light versus 2.6 with fake light (difference 7.2 points, 95% CI 2.4–12.1, p=0.011) in 30 children over 8 weeks.  
**Methodology:** Randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial of 30 children ages 2–6 over 8 weeks, published in a peer-reviewed journal and funded by the device company, with company founders and employees among the authors.  
**Researchers:** Yuliy Fradkin, L. De Taboada, M. Naeser, A. Saltmarche, W. Snyder, E. Steingold, Stefano Pallanti (2022 retrospective study)  
**Institutes:** Two private medical offices in Brooklyn and Manhattan, New York (main trial), JelikaLite Corp. (device maker and funder), Istituto di Neuroscienze, Florence, Italy (2022 study), Mass General Brigham (registered adult trial)  

## What this research found

- The idea has a layered history. Lab work found that near-infrared light is absorbed by the part of a cell that makes energy (the mitochondria). Animal studies and small human studies in stroke, brain injury and depression followed. Autism studies began around 2018. A study of about 40 people compared a red laser with a very weak placebo light and reported symptom improvement. A follow-up reported lower irritability six months later. A small open-label study of about 10 intellectually capable autistic adults, where everyone knew they were getting real light, reported milder symptoms, with one headache and one case of trouble sleeping. Those earlier reports come from a trial registry listing and the 2024 trial paper, and their full reports were not reviewed here.
- A 2022 Italian review of clinic records covered 21 children ages 5 to 15. They used two light devices (a headset and a nose-worn light) at home, five days a week for six months. Scores on autism-related behaviors, rigidity, attention, sleep and parenting stress all improved. There was no comparison group, and the parents who ran the sessions also filled out most of the rating forms. Two children (about 10%) had occasional headaches.
- In the 2024 trial, 14 of 16 children (87%) on real light improved by a clinically meaningful amount, compared with 5 of 14 (35%) on fake light. Parents' weekly ratings of eight everyday behaviors, such as new words, sleep, eye contact and tantrums, also favored real light, but only just (p=0.05). Brain-wave recordings from 17 children hinted at a shift away from slow delta waves and toward theta waves with real light. This was exploratory and did not reach the usual threshold for a firm result.
- No serious adverse events were reported. Within the first three weeks, four children on real light became overexcited, two had mild headaches that faded, and one had more tics than usual. Some also seemed more irritable. Overall side effects were not significantly more common than with the fake light. One child in the fake-light group left after the first session, after what looked like a possible seizure that was never confirmed.

## What this means for caregivers

- Light therapy for autism is at a promising but unproven stage. It is backed by one small controlled trial and several studies without comparison groups. The trial tested one investigational device, with a set light type, pulse rate and session length, given in a clinic. It cannot tell us whether other light devices, such as home red-light panels, would do the same. The researchers say the best dose is still unknown. The device received an FDA Breakthrough Device Designation in 2021, which is not the same as approval.
- Researchers say the next steps are larger randomized trials, studies that compare different doses, longer follow-up, and brain imaging. Light may reach the brain differently in children with darker skin or thicker skulls, so dosing may need to differ. A randomized adult trial of 41 people is registered at Mass General Brigham, but no results were seen. Until trials like these report, the questions about benefit and safety stay open.
- Children on regular medication or with a seizure history were left out of the main trial, so its findings do not cover them. Your child's doctor knows their health history and can help you weigh any device before you try it.

## Important caveats

- The trial was small and short: 30 children, 8 weeks, no follow-up afterward, at two offices in New York. Four children were replaced during the study. One child randomized to fake light was mistakenly given real treatment and counted in the real-light group. Most children were White, and those with seizures or on regular medication were excluded.
- The device maker, JelikaLite, paid for the study and helped with its design, data collection and analysis, and the decision to publish. Some authors are company founders, employees or equity holders. The paper lists the last author, who did the blinded symptom ratings, as a company co-founder. Independent teams have not yet repeated the results.
- The two groups were not perfectly matched. The real-light group had more nonverbal children (9 of 16 versus 3 of 14) and slightly higher starting scores. Many children were also in intensive therapy. The fake-light group improved by 2.6 points too, which shows how much scores can change for other reasons. Parents' reports can be shaped by hope.
- Most earlier reports had no fake-light comparison, and several came from the groups selling or developing the devices. Some parent-advocacy websites call light therapy 'evidence-based and safe' or describe 'marked improvements'. That goes further than these studies support.

## A Curioler thought

Every brain is a small universe of cells, each turning fuel into the energy that powers a thought. Science is only beginning to ask whether gentle light might touch that quiet machinery, and each careful answer opens new questions behind it. There is something wonderful in how much is still unknown about the minds we love.
