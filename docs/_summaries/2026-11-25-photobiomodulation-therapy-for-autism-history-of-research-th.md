## At a glance

**Sample size:** 30 children in the 2024 randomized trial (16 real light, 14 fake light); 22 children finished the 2025 open-label follow-up (25 enrolled)  
**Demographics:** Autistic children ages 2–7 in New York City. In the 2024 trial, mean age about 4.6–4.9; 24 boys and 6 girls; 22 of 30 White; 18 verbal and 12 non-verbal; most had moderate-to-severe autism ratings. Children with a seizure history or regular medication were excluded. The 2025 study had mean age 4.95, 19 boys and 3 girls, 86% White, and 16 verbal children.  
**Key outcome:** Children who got real light had a 7.23-point larger drop in their CARS-2 autism rating than the fake-light group (95% CI 2.36–12.11, p=0.011).  
**Methodology:** A randomized, double-blind trial with a look-alike fake device in 30 children, plus a single-group follow-up in 22 children, both published in peer-reviewed journals with the device maker involved.  
**Researchers:** Yuliy Fradkin, Eugenia Steingold, Luis De Taboada, Margaret Naeser, Anne Saltmarche, Wayne Snyder, Joaquin A. Anguera, Alexander J. Simon  
**Institutes:** JelikaLite Corp (device maker, New York), Rutgers University (author contact address; Statistics Department helped analyze EEG data)  

## What this research found

**The idea.** Photobiomodulation is a long word for a simple idea: shining gentle light on the body to change how cells work. It goes back to 1967, when a low-power laser made hair grow faster on mice. Since then, scientists have tried light on the head for brain injury, stroke, depression and dementia. In 2022, Catherine Hamilton, John Mitrofanis and colleagues wrote a review arguing it might help in autism too. Their reasoning starts in the mitochondria, the tiny power plants inside every cell, which soak up this kind of light. They suggest the light could give brain cells more energy, calm inflammation in the brain, and help distant parts of the brain work together. They called it an idea worth testing.

**The first tries in people.** In 2022, a pilot study of 11 autistic adults without an intellectual disability reported fewer autism traits after eight weeks of flashing light on the forehead. A group led by Calixto Machado reported that children were less irritable after four weeks of low-power laser treatment, and the 2022 review says the change lasted up to 12 months. The same year, a look back at the records of children treated in Italy reported lower autism ratings, less rigid behavior and better sleep.

**What the 2024 study tested.** Then came the first study with a fair comparison. A team led by Yuliy Fradkin and Eugenia Steingold tested their own soft, wireless cap, called Cognilum, at two offices in New York City in 2021. It weighs about a pound and has six small lights that flash invisible light 40 times a second. The lights point at brain areas linked to social thinking, at two areas that handle language, and at the back of the head. Thirty children ages 2 to 6 were split into two groups by chance. One group wore the real cap. The other wore a cap that looked and felt the same but gave off no light. Sessions were twice a week for eight weeks. The light time was built up slowly to 5 or 6 minutes, so children would not get overexcited, and children could walk around, play or watch cartoons while they wore it. Neither the families nor the specialist who scored the children knew who had the real cap. About three in four children kept up their usual therapies.

**What changed.** The specialist's rating gives a lower score when autism traits are fewer or milder. In the real-cap group, the average score dropped from 43.5 to 33.7. In the no-light group, it dropped from 40.6 to 38.0. The researchers counted a drop of 4.5 points or more as a meaningful change. By that measure, 14 of the 16 children with the real cap improved (87%), compared with 5 of the 14 with the no-light cap (35%). Every week, staff who did not know which cap a child had phoned the parents, and parents reported more good changes in the real-cap group. Some children had mild side effects. The authors say those parents chose to keep going because they saw better eye contact, better focus, and better understanding and use of words.

**A look at brain waves.** Seventeen of the 30 children were able to wear a second cap with sensors that record brain waves, the brain's electrical activity. In the real-cap group, slow brain waves tended to fall and slightly faster ones tended to rise, more than in the no-light group. The children whose scores improved most tended to show the biggest shift. The authors think this may be an early sign of what the light does in the brain, and say it needs much more study.

**The 2025 follow-up and what families noticed.** The same team then gave the real cap to 22 more children, ages 2 to 7, for eight weeks. This time every child got real light. Their average score fell from 36.45 to 29.68, about the same drop the real-cap group had in 2024. Their brain waves shifted too, with fast waves rising and slow waves falling. The authors noticed that some of the changes families cared about most never showed up on the rating at all, like holding a parent's hand to cross the street, or using the bathroom on their own.

**Where it goes next.** The researchers and reviewers agree on what comes next. They want bigger, careful studies run by teams outside the company. They want to find the right dose: how fast the light flashes, how long each session lasts, and for how many weeks. The 2024 team thinks older children and children with darker skin may need longer sessions, because more of the light is soaked up before it reaches the brain. They also want to follow children for longer, look inside the brain with scans, measure everyday behavior and family life, and check how the light mixes with medicines. Hamilton's team wants animal studies to learn why the light might work. They also float ideas like a light helmet worn every day, and effects that might start in the gut. For now, the light looks worth testing carefully.

## What this means for caregivers

- Light therapy for autism is an early research idea, not a proven treatment. The first studies show signs worth following up. They can't tell us yet whether it really helps, or for how long.
- The studies tested one cap, at set settings, in short sessions run by a research team. They don't show that the red-light caps, panels or mats sold for home use work the same way.
- In the studies, children kept their usual therapies, so what's being tested is light added to them. If you're thinking about trying it, your child's pediatrician or developmental specialist is the right person to talk it through with.

## Important caveats

- **Small and early.** Only one study compared real light with a fake cap, and it had just 30 children from two New York offices, most of them White. The 2025 follow-up gave every child real light, so there was nothing to compare it with.
- **Run by the company that makes the cap.** JelikaLite paid for the 2024 study and helped design and analyze it. Some of the authors work for the company or own part of it. No outside team has repeated the results yet.
- **Other things could explain some of the change.** The children who got real light started with higher scores, and more of them did not yet use spoken words. Children also grow, keep up their usual therapies, and parents hope. The study lasted only eight weeks, so no one knows if any change lasts. The brain-wave results are early hints, not proof.
- **Side effects were mild, but only checked for a short time.** Some children became overexcited or had headaches. In the 2025 study, most parents saw their child become overactive at least once. Children with seizures or on regular medicine were left out, so the results do not tell us about them.

## A Curioler thought

Every brain is a small universe of cells, each one turning fuel into the energy behind a thought. Science is only starting to ask whether gentle light can reach that quiet work. We don't know yet, and that is part of the wonder: there is still so much to learn about the minds we love.

*Be curious, keep exploring.*
