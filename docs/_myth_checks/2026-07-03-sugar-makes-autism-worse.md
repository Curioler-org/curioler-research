## The claim

Many caregivers of autistic children avoid sugar — or are advised to — on the belief that it worsens autism symptoms. Social media is full of accounts from parents who say cutting sugar transformed their child's behaviour. The claim feels intuitive: sugar spikes energy, disrupts sleep, and upsets the gut. Could it also be amplifying autistic traits?

## What the evidence says

- **Sugar does not cause or worsen core autism traits.** Social communication differences, sensory sensitivities, and repetitive behaviours are rooted in neurodevelopment — specifically in genetics and brain structure differences that emerge before birth. No dietary study has shown that sugar intake influences these foundational traits. Controlled trials in neurotypical children have repeatedly shown that sugar does not cause behavioural changes, and no equivalent study shows it drives core autism characteristics.

- **But sugar is linked to worse co-occurring difficulties.** This is the important nuance. A 2022 study in *Frontiers in Nutrition* (107 autistic children, 207 controls) found that autistic children consuming more than two servings of sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) per week had significantly worse emotional control and planning/organisation than those who did not. A separate 2022 study found SSB intake was associated with poorer motor function in autistic children. These are co-occurring difficulties — not core autism — but they are real and they affect daily life significantly.

- **The gut-brain axis is the most credible mechanism.** High sugar intake disrupts the gut microbiome — reducing microbial diversity and promoting dysbiosis. Multiple systematic reviews confirm that autistic children already have distinct gut microbiota profiles. Sugar likely amplifies this disruption, which may in turn affect mood, attention, and behaviour via the gut-brain axis. This is supported by trials of probiotic interventions showing modest improvements in co-occurring behavioural symptoms.

- **The 'sugar causes hyperactivity' myth has been debunked.** Double-blind controlled trials consistently find no direct link between sugar consumption and hyperactivity — in autistic or neurotypical children. Where parents perceive more hyperactivity after sugar, it is typically explained by expectation bias (they *expected* the child to be more active). This does not mean sugar has no effects; it means hyperactivity specifically is not the mechanism.

- **Selective eating complicates the picture.** Many autistic children have significant food selectivity driven by sensory sensitivities. Ultra-processed, sweeter foods are often among the few foods they will accept. Studies showing higher sugar intake in autistic children may partly reflect this — the sugar is a downstream consequence of restricted eating, not an independent dietary choice. This means the causal arrow may run in multiple directions.

## The verdict explained

The statement is misleading because "makes autism worse" implies sugar affects the core features of autism — and it does not. But buried in that imprecise claim is a real and important finding: high sugar intake, particularly from sweetened beverages, is consistently associated with worse emotional regulation, executive function, and motor skills in autistic children. These are not trivial difficulties — for many families they are among the hardest parts of daily life.

Reducing sugar-sweetened drinks in particular is a low-risk, evidence-consistent step. It won't change your child's autism, but it may support the co-occurring challenges that sit alongside it.

## What to do with this

- **Cutting SSBs (sugary drinks) is reasonable** — the association with emotional regulation and executive function is the most consistent finding in autism-specific dietary research
- **Don't expect dietary changes to reduce core autism traits** — if that's the goal, evidence-based interventions (communication therapy, OT, ABA, etc.) have a much stronger evidence base
- **Consider the whole diet rather than singling out sugar** — high sugar intake is usually part of a broader ultra-processed dietary pattern; overall diet quality matters more than any single nutrient
- **Discuss any significant dietary changes with your child's paediatrician or a dietitian**, especially if your child is a selective eater — nutritional adequacy is a real concern in this group
