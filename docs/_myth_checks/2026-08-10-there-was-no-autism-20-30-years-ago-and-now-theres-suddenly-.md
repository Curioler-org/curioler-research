## The claim

People point to numbers like the CDC's 1-in-31 (2022) figure, compare it to almost no diagnoses a few decades ago, and conclude that something new must suddenly be causing far more children to develop autism — language often summed up as an 'autism epidemic' or 'pandemic.'

## What the evidence says

- CDC surveillance shows a real rise in diagnosed prevalence: about 1 in 150 8-year-olds in the earliest full ADDM Network report (children born 1992) to 1 in 31 in the most recent report (children born 2014) — a genuine, measured increase in how many children are identified.
- Before 2000, the U.S. had no systematic nationwide monitoring of autism at all; 1995 estimates put prevalence around 1 in 500 using much narrower criteria, and the 1960s–80s figures (roughly 0.5–1 per 1,000) reflect a far stricter, rarely-applied definition — so 'no autism back then' largely reflects a lack of looking, not a lack of autistic people.
- The diagnostic category itself changed size: DSM-III-R and DSM-IV (1994) broadened the criteria, and DSM-5 (2013) folded Asperger's disorder and PDD-NOS into one umbrella 'autism spectrum disorder' diagnosis, which by itself pulled in many people who previously had a different or no diagnosis.
- Epidemiologists who run the CDC surveillance (e.g., Johns Hopkins' Christine Ladd-Acosta) attribute the rise mainly to broadened diagnostic definitions plus large increases in screening and public awareness — not a sudden epidemic-style outbreak. Notably, prevalence among the subgroup with the most severe, high-support needs has increased only minimally over the last decade; nearly all of the recent rise is in milder, more subtly-presenting cases that clinicians are now better at recognizing.
- The once-popular vaccine/thimerosal explanation for a sudden rise does not hold up: thimerosal was removed from childhood vaccines by 2001, yet diagnosed autism rates kept climbing steadily afterward in the U.S. and other countries that removed it, and large studies have found no causal link.

## The verdict explained

It's true and well-documented that the number of children diagnosed with autism has risen sharply over the past few decades — that part of the claim is accurate and shows up consistently in CDC data. But the framing that autism 'didn't exist' before and that there is now a 'pandemic' implies a sudden real-world outbreak of a new condition, and that part isn't supported by the evidence. Autism as a condition has always existed; what changed is how narrowly it was defined, how hard clinicians and researchers looked for it, and how willing families and systems were to identify and label it. Diagnostic manuals broadened the category multiple times (most dramatically in 2013), screening at pediatric wellness visits expanded, awareness grew enormously, and systematic nationwide counting only began around 2000. Each of those alone would raise the count without a single additional child actually being autistic.

This doesn't mean the increase is entirely an artifact of counting, or that no genetic or environmental research is worth doing — legitimate scientists continue to study contributing factors, since autism is understood to arise from a mix of genetic and environmental influences. But the specific idea of a sudden, mysterious 'pandemic' striking today's children in a way that never happened before is not what the epidemiological data shows; researchers who track the numbers describe a gradual, explainable rise rather than an epidemic-style event. This is also a politically contested framing — some public officials have publicly described it as a genuine unexplained epidemic, while most epidemiologists studying the same data attribute the trend primarily to how autism is defined and detected.

## What this means for caregivers

- A rising diagnosis count is not evidence that something is newly 'wrong' with today's children — many autistic adults from earlier generations were simply never identified, misdiagnosed, or given a different label.
- Broader, more inclusive diagnostic criteria are generally good news for families: they mean more children (and adults) can access early intervention, school services, and support that wasn't available to previous generations.
- Claims of a sudden autism 'pandemic' with an unidentified single trigger should be treated with caution — the well-documented drivers (diagnostic changes, screening, awareness) already explain most of the trend.

## Important caveats

- This verdict addresses the *rate of increase*, not the ultimate causes of autism itself — autism's underlying causes are still an active and legitimate area of genetic and environmental research.
- Because pre-2000 U.S. data is sparse and used inconsistent, narrower definitions, precise historical comparisons are inherently uncertain — researchers cannot fully separate 'more autistic children' from 'better counting' with 100% confidence, even though the available evidence points strongly toward counting/definition changes as the dominant factor.
