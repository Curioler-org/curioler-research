## The claim

Homeopathy — a system of medicine using substances diluted to extremely low concentrations, based on the principle that 'like cures like' — is sometimes promoted to parents as a gentle, natural, effective treatment for autism, particularly for those wary of conventional medication or seeking options beyond standard therapies.

## What the evidence says

- A systematic review of homeopathy for conditions common in intellectual disability found only one clinical trial that specifically tested homeopathy in autism — a small 2001 pilot study of homeopathic secretin in 12 children. Two independent reviewers rated it poor quality using standard trial-quality scoring, and the study itself reported no improvement in the children treated.
- That review's overall conclusion for autism was blunt: 'there is no evidence in favor or against the effectiveness of homeopathy for the management of Autistic Spectrum Disorder' — meaning the evidence base is essentially empty, not merely mixed or preliminary.
- A separate, comprehensive review of complementary and alternative treatments for autism spectrum disorder — which specifically catalogued treatments with at least some positive research evidence — did not include homeopathy among them at all.
- Beyond autism specifically, the broader scientific consensus on homeopathy is unambiguous: a 2015 comprehensive review by Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC), evaluating the totality of rigorous clinical trials across all conditions studied, concluded 'there are no health conditions for which there is reliable evidence that homeopathy is effective' — a position echoed by the U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH).
- Homeopathy's core principle — that a substance diluted to the point where often no molecules of the original ingredient remain can still produce a specific therapeutic effect — has no established biological mechanism and conflicts with basic chemistry and pharmacology, which helps explain why it has consistently failed to outperform placebo in rigorous trials across many conditions.

## The verdict explained

'Very effective' is about as strong a claim as a treatment claim can make, and the evidence points the opposite direction. For autism specifically, researchers who systematically searched for supporting trials found essentially nothing to work with — a single small, poor-quality study that itself reported no benefit. That isn't 'insufficient evidence leaning positive' — it's the closest thing to a null result available in the one study that actually tested it.

Zooming out, this fits a consistent pattern across the wider scientific literature on homeopathy: government-level reviews in Australia and evaluations by U.S. health authorities have both concluded that no health condition — not just autism — has reliable evidence that homeopathy works better than placebo. Combined with the lack of a plausible biological mechanism for how extreme dilutions could produce a specific therapeutic effect, 'homeopathy is very effective in autism' is a claim well outside what the evidence supports.

## What this means for caregivers

- If a practitioner or product claims strong, specific effectiveness for autism, treat that as a red flag rather than reassurance — the actual research doesn't back that framing.
- Using homeopathy alongside evidence-based support isn't necessarily physically harmful in itself (most homeopathic remedies are highly diluted), but relying on it in place of things with real evidence — speech therapy, occupational therapy, behavioral support — can mean losing valuable time during a child's most responsive developmental years.
- It's reasonable to ask any practitioner recommending homeopathy for autism what published, controlled evidence they're basing that recommendation on.

## Important caveats

- Most homeopathic preparations carry low direct physical risk because they're so diluted — the concern here is wasted time, money, and false hope rather than toxicity.
- Absence of evidence for a therapeutic effect doesn't mean a child can't happen to improve while using homeopathy — improvement can come from natural development, concurrent therapies, or expectation effects, and shouldn't be mistaken for the homeopathy itself working.
