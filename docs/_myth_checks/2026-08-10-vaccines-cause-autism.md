## The claim

The idea traces back to a 1998 paper by Andrew Wakefield in The Lancet linking the MMR vaccine to autism, and separately to concerns that thimerosal (a mercury-based preservative once used in some vaccines) might be a cause. Both ideas spread widely and still circulate today, including recent political statements reviving the claim.

## What the evidence says

- Wakefield's original 1998 study — the source of the MMR-autism claim — was retracted by The Lancet in 2010 after investigation found he had falsified data and had undisclosed financial conflicts of interest; he was subsequently stripped of his medical license in the UK.
- A 2014 meta-analysis pooling over 1.2 million children across multiple cohort and case-control studies found no connection between vaccines and autism, including no link to the MMR vaccine, thimerosal, or mercury exposure.
- Large national studies specifically targeting the MMR vaccine found no increased autism risk: a 2015 JAMA study of over 95,000 U.S. children (including those with an older sibling with autism, a higher-risk group) and a Danish study of over 650,000 children both found no association.
- Thimerosal was removed from most U.S. childhood vaccines by 2001, yet autism diagnosis rates kept rising afterward — in the U.S. and in other countries like Denmark and Sweden that removed it earlier — which contradicts a causal role for thimerosal.
- A 2021 systematic review covering 338 studies on routine childhood vaccines found no link between the MMR vaccine and autism.
- In November 2025, the CDC signaled a shift in its official position on vaccines and autism under HHS leadership; medical organizations including the National Medical Association publicly stated that no credible new evidence supports a causal link and that the change reflects policy, not new science.

## The verdict explained

Vaccines causing autism is about as thoroughly tested a medical claim as exists, and it does not hold up. The scare began with a single 1998 study of just 12 children, which was later found to be fraudulent — the lead author manipulated data and had an undisclosed financial interest in the outcome, and the paper was formally retracted. Since then, researchers around the world have repeatedly tried to find a link using much larger, better-designed studies, including several that specifically tested the highest-risk groups (children with an older autistic sibling). None of them found one. When mercury-based thimerosal was blamed instead, that explanation also failed the basic test: autism rates kept climbing steadily in the years after thimerosal was removed from vaccines.

What is true is that autism symptoms are often first noticed around the same age children receive several routine vaccines (roughly 12-24 months), which creates an understandable but misleading impression of a connection — two things happening around the same time isn't evidence that one caused the other. It's also true that in late 2025, U.S. federal health leadership publicly revived claims of a link; but this reflects a political and administrative decision, not a body of new scientific evidence, and has been contested by mainstream medical organizations. The scientific consensus, built on decades of independent research across many countries, remains that vaccines do not cause autism.

## What this means for caregivers

- Delaying or skipping recommended vaccines to avoid autism does not reduce autism risk, but it does leave a child vulnerable to measles, mumps, rubella, and other serious preventable diseases.
- If your child was later diagnosed with autism after a vaccination, that timing reflects when signs of autism are typically first noticed by parents and pediatricians (usually in the second year of life), not a causal trigger.
- It's reasonable to have questions about vaccine ingredients or schedules — raising them with your pediatrician is different from acting on the specific, well-debunked autism claim.

## Important caveats

- This verdict is about whether vaccines cause autism, not about vaccine side effects in general (like the known, temporary risk of febrile seizures after MMR) — those are separate, real, and well-documented but unrelated to autism.
- Research into the genetic and environmental factors that do contribute to autism is active and ongoing; ruling out vaccines doesn't mean the causes of autism are fully understood.
