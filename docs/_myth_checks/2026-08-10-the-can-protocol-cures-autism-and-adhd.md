## The claim

The 'C.A.N. (Cure Autism Now) Protocol' is promoted by Biswaroop Roy Chowdhury (marketed as 'Dr. BRC') through clinics, social media, and a paid 'Cure Autism Now Kit,' claiming it is a home-based, drug-less protocol that has 'helped 6000+ children come out of the trap of Autism/ADHD' — implying autism and ADHD can be reversed or cured.

## What the evidence says

- Biswaroop Roy Chowdhury has no formal medical qualifications or training. Wikipedia and independent journalism document that his 'PhD' is an honorary degree from Alliance International University in Zambia, an institution that is unaccredited and has since been deregistered.
- He has a documented history of making false health claims before this one: the Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI) formally ruled in 2016 and 2017 that his advertisements claiming a 'Diabetes Type 1 & Type 2 Cure in 72 Hrs' were false and grossly misleading.
- He gained wider notoriety during the COVID-19 pandemic for promoting health misinformation; the BBC profiled him under the headline 'The rise of India's Covid quack,' and he has faced a criminal complaint alleging he falsely claimed to be a medical practitioner and offered fraudulent treatment.
- No independent, peer-reviewed clinical trial or published study of the C.A.N. Protocol exists. The '6000+ children helped' figure and testimonials come exclusively from the promoter's own marketing (social media posts, sales pages, and a paid certification course), with no independent verification of outcomes.
- Mainstream autism research and clinical organizations consistently describe autism as a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference, not a disease — there is no known cure. Legitimate, evidence-based support (speech therapy, occupational therapy, behavioral intervention) focuses on building skills and quality of life, not reversal.

## The verdict explained

This isn't a case of mixed evidence — it follows the same pattern as other unproven 'autism cure' products that autism researchers and clinicians have warned families about for years, from chelation to bleach-based 'MMS' therapy to the earlier 'DAN!' protocol. The person marketing the C.A.N. Protocol has no verifiable medical training, uses an honorary degree from an unaccredited, now-deregistered institution to present himself as a doctor, and has already been officially found to have made a false 'cure' claim about a different condition (diabetes) before turning to autism and ADHD. The only evidence offered for the protocol's effectiveness is the promoter's own social media testimonials and sales material — there is no independent study, no peer review, and no data anyone outside his organization can check.

Combined with the broader scientific consensus that autism is not a disease with a cure, but a different way the brain develops, a specific commercial protocol claiming to 'cure' it — sold as a kit with a hotline for enrollment — has all the hallmarks of a health scam rather than a legitimate treatment. Families should treat this claim as false and be wary of any program that promises to reverse or cure autism, especially one sold as a product.

## What this means for caregivers

- Be skeptical of any product, kit, or protocol that promises to 'cure' or 'reverse' autism — this is one of the clearest red flags for a non-evidence-based treatment.
- Check credentials before trusting health claims: an honorary or unaccredited 'PhD' is not the same as medical training, and a prior finding of false advertising (as happened here with diabetes claims) is a strong warning sign.
- Evidence-based therapies — speech and language therapy, occupational therapy, applied behavior analysis, and other individualized supports — don't promise a cure, but they do have research backing for improving specific skills and quality of life.

## Important caveats

- Children with autism do sometimes show real improvement over time due to normal development, maturation, or other concurrent therapies — this can create a false impression that an unrelated product caused the change.
- This verdict is specific to the C.A.N. Protocol and its promoter's claims and credentials; it isn't a judgment on all dietary, naturopathic, or complementary approaches in general, though none have been shown to cure autism.
